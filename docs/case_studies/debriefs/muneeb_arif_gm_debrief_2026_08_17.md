## 2. How to read this document (no jargon)

For each assignment I give you four things in plain language:

1. **What he wrote** — in normal English, short.
2. **Why he wrote it that way** — the thinking behind the choice.
3. **Why that's good (or not) — and *why* it's good.** This is the part you asked for. I never
   just say "strong"; I say what it would have looked like if he'd got it wrong, so you can see
   the difference.
4. **What to ask him** — questions you can read out loud.

A word you'll see a lot: **register**. It just means *who he is writing to and how he treats
their power.* An email to your boss and an email to your friend say the same thing in different
registers. In this case study, register is the whole test — because a Growth Manager who talks to
a government Secretary the way you talk to a donor will fail, no matter how smart they are.

## 3. Assignment 1 — The one-pager for the Secretary

### What he wrote

Title: *Strengthening Government Education: From Training to Classroom Support.*

His argument in one line: **government already spends on curriculum, textbooks, training,
monitoring and digital tools — the problem is that none of it reliably reaches the actual
classroom.** He draws the missing chain as
*Training → Lesson Preparation → Teaching → Feedback → Assessment → Improvement*, says the gap is
in the *connection*, not in "more training," and positions Taleemabad as the thing that
strengthens that chain rather than a new layer on top of it.

He then cites Taleemabad's evidence, proposes a **30-day joint assessment** instead of a rollout,
states that **government keeps ownership** of policy, teachers, institutions, data and scale
decisions, and closes with a single **Decision Requested**: which priority, and which unit leads
the 30-day assessment.

### Why he wrote it that way

He worked out what the reader is afraid of. A Secretary of School Education isn't short of
ambition — he's short of cover. He's been burned by pilots that ended when the money ended, by
tablets in cupboards, by launches that generated headlines and no learning. So Muneeb's whole
document is engineered to lower perceived risk: small first step, government in charge, nothing
irreversible, one decision to make.

The "not adding another layer of bureaucracy" line and "making teachers' work easier" tell you he
also thought about the second audience — the officials and teachers who'd have to *carry* this.

### Why that's good — and why

Compare it to what almost everyone writes. **The standard version opens with Pakistan's learning
crisis**: out-of-school children, learning poverty, a national tragedy, then "Taleemabad is the
answer." It reads well to us. To the Secretary it reads as a stranger explaining his own job back
to him, badly — and it's a subtle insult, because *he* is the person accountable for that crisis.

Muneeb never does this. He opens by **crediting** what government already invests, and frames the
problem as a *connection* failure rather than a *government* failure. That is the difference
between "you have a problem" and "you have assets that aren't compounding" — and only the second
one gets you a second meeting.

Four more things that are quietly excellent:

- **"The gap is therefore not simply more training."** This is the *turn* — the sentence that
  changes the reader's mind. Weak memos have no turn; they just describe. His turn also
  pre-empts the obvious objection ("we already do teacher training").
- **He asks for a 30-day joint assessment, not a pilot.** Small, cheap, reversible, and it makes
  government a co-author of the diagnosis rather than a recipient of a proposal. People defend
  what they helped write.
- **"Government would retain ownership of policy, teachers, institutions, data and decisions on
  scale."** He volunteered the thing officials are most anxious about — losing control, and
  especially losing data — before being asked. That is a trust move, and it costs him nothing.
- **He ends with one decision, not a menu.** "Which priority, and which unit leads?" A Secretary
  can answer that in the meeting. "We'd love to explore a partnership" cannot be answered at all,
  which is why most pitches die politely.

### The one thing to check before you trust it

He cites specific figures: **422 public schools in Islamabad, ~4,000 teachers, ~90,000 students,
a 0.28 standard-deviation learning effect, and gains of 21pp in Urdu, 16pp in Maths, 4pp in
English.** He also cites Balochistan numbers (1,230 students, 104 teachers; a Winter School
programme across 60 schools, 800 teachers, 8,000 students) and, in Assignment 2, classroom
observation figures (522 AI observations, 54 human, 6% student talk time, 87% closed-ended
questions).

**I cannot verify these. You can.** Two possibilities and they are very different:

- If they're our real published figures → he did genuinely good research, and his handling is
  *exemplary*: he writes "Taleemabad **reports** a quasi-experimental evaluation" and "These are
  Taleemabad's **reported** evaluation findings," and in Assignment 2 he adds "Evidence claims
  should be rechecked against Taleemabad's current published study materials before external
  circulation." That is a person who knows the difference between a figure he owns and a figure he
  is repeating.
- If any number is invented → that is disqualifying, and no amount of hedging fixes it.

**Please put these in front of whoever owns the study before the debrief.** It's a 10-minute
check that changes the verdict.

### Questions to ask

1. "Walk me through the one-pager as if I'm the Secretary and I've given you three minutes. Go."
   — *Listen for whether he leads with the risk-and-proof argument or reverts to the mission.*
2. "He reads it and says: *this is exactly what the last three organisations told me.* What do you
   say next?" — *The whole role is in this answer. Good: more evidence, or a smaller ask. Bad:
   more enthusiasm.*
3. "Where did the 0.28 SD figure come from, and how confident are you in it?" — *Ask even though
   he hedged it well. You're testing whether he knows what he doesn't know.*
4. "Your ask is a 30-day joint assessment. He says yes in principle but nobody has time. Now
   what?" — *Tests whether the small ask was strategy or a formula.*
5. "Which of your five slides is the one where he changes his mind — and what happens if he
   doesn't?" — *You need this one because I couldn't read the slides.*

## 4. Assignment 2 — The Room

### What he wrote

A **closed-door working session**, 105 minutes, deliberately small and senior. Explicitly *not* a
launch: "This should not be a marketing launch."

The evidence is staged as a progression — **Islamabad** (did students learn more?) → **Balochistan**
(what's happening inside classrooms?) → **Punjab** (can government systems carry this at scale?),
which he is careful to label an *active scale test, not completed impact evidence*.

Eleven attendee types with a distinct walk-away thought for each: federal leadership, FDE, Sindh /
Punjab / Balochistan education leadership, Planning & Finance, assessment and research bodies
(ASER/ITA, CERP, LUMS School of Education), development partners, foundations/implementers, and a
small number of sector voices.

Agenda highlights: opens with **one classroom story + one policy question**; an **Independent Red
Team** slot where an outside researcher names the strongest claim, the claim needing most caution,
and the biggest unanswered question; a **Government Dialogue** built on one question — *"What
would break if this moved from evidence to scale?"*; then three parallel working conversations;
then a **Commitments** round where each group names one insight, one open question and one next
conversation, captured as institution / person / issue / owner / meeting date.

Three leadership talking points: *Access is not the same as learning* · *The technology is not the
intervention, the feedback loop is* · *Scale is not replication, scale is adaptation.*

Follow-up: evidence brief to everyone in 24 hours (including **what the evidence does not
demonstrate**); within 7 days, **in-person first for government**, with a rule — "Return with
something useful... Keep regular check-ins substantive rather than repeatedly asking for
procurement or approvals." Every relationship logged as
**SIGNAL → INSTITUTIONAL FIT → NEXT QUESTION → OWNER → MEETING DATE → OPPORTUNITY**. And the
explicit statement that the commercial outcome should *not* be immediate procurement.

His design principle, verbatim: **"Do not fill the room with people who already like
Taleemabad."**

### Why he wrote it that way

He understood that "thought leadership" is not publicity — it's **credibility among peers**, and
credibility is only earned in front of people who could plausibly disagree with you. Every
structural choice follows from that: small room, red team, a dialogue question inviting attack,
and stating the study's limits before anyone else can.

The in-person-first follow-up rule tells you something about his actual experience: he knows that
with government, email documents a relationship but does not build one.

### Why that's good — and why

This is the strongest of the four, and here's the contrast that shows why.

**The typical answer is a launch event.** Chief guest, keynote from leadership, media wall, press
release, a panel of friendly voices, a hashtag. It looks like success, generates photos, and
produces nothing — because everyone in the room already agreed, and nobody left with an obligation.

Muneeb inverts nearly every one of those choices:

- **He invites scrutiny on purpose.** The Red Team slot is the single best idea in the pack.
  Paying an outside researcher to attack your own study in front of federal and provincial
  leadership is a *confidence signal* — and it's how a room full of technical people ends up
  trusting you. Most candidates would never risk it. His line "Taleemabad can demonstrate
  confidence by inviting scrutiny" shows he knows exactly why he's doing it.
- **"What would break if this moved from evidence to scale?"** is the right question because it
  makes senior officials the *experts* in the room instead of the audience. People commit to
  things they helped stress-test. It also surfaces the real objections while you're standing
  there, rather than six weeks later in a file you never see.
- **Each attendee type has a different walk-away.** Weak room maps list job titles and give
  everyone the same takeaway ("Taleemabad is credible"). His Planning & Finance walk-away is
  about affordability and recurrent cost; his researchers' walk-away is about independent
  interrogation. That means he knows these people want different things — which is the entire
  skill of convening.
- **The follow-up engine is the real deliverable and he treats it that way.** Most submissions
  spend 80% on the agenda and two bullets on follow-up. His is differentiated per stakeholder,
  in-person first, has a rule against nagging, and defines pipeline as three pathways
  (government adoption / independent validation / scale financing) rather than one procurement.
- **He states the study's limits, twice, unprompted.** "What the evidence demonstrates / does not
  demonstrate." Two districts is not a national claim, and a room of researchers will say so —
  better that we say it first. Volunteering your own weakness in front of the people who'd find
  it is how credibility actually gets built.
- **"The commercial outcome should not be immediate procurement."** He is deliberately slowing
  down the sale. Only people who have actually closed institutional deals do this, because they
  know that pushing procurement in the room is what gets you politely removed from the network.

**The one soft spot:** the room is *big* in seniority — four provinces plus federal plus finance
plus researchers plus donors in one 105-minute session. That's a lot of competing agendas. He
never says which two attendees actually matter most. Probe it.

### Questions to ask

1. "Budget's halved. The room is 15 people, not 60. Who stays, and who do you cut?" — *Forces the
   prioritisation his brief avoids. A strong answer already knows its two people.*
2. "Your Red Team researcher says on stage that the effect size is inflated by selection bias.
   Federal Secretary is watching. What do you do in that moment?" — *He designed this risk. Make
   him live in it. Watch for concede-and-reframe versus defend-or-deflect.*
3. "Four provinces in one room — walk me through how you stop it becoming a photo-op with
   speeches." — *Tests whether the anti-speech mechanisms are real or decorative.*
4. "It's day 3 after the event. Who are you with, and what are you asking them for?" — *Follow-up
   engines written to fill a section collapse here.*
5. "You wrote 'don't fill the room with people who already like Taleemabad.' Who's the person you'd
   most want in the room who probably doesn't like us?" — *Excellent culture read. Watch whether
   he'll actually name someone or retreat to the abstract.*

## 5. Assignment 3 — The Pipeline

### What he wrote

His thesis: Punjab already has all the machinery — policy authority, teacher-development bodies,
assessment institutions, PPP delivery platforms, development planning, external financing. So the
opportunity is to **plug into an existing government problem and an existing route**, not to
introduce another standalone digital intervention.

Five partners, chosen so that each one controls a *different link in the chain*
(policy → technical design → financing/approval → implementation → evidence):

| Partner | His logic | His opening question |
|---|---|---|
| Punjab School Education Department | The system owner | "Where does the Department lose visibility between teacher support, classroom practice and learning?" |
| Punjab Education Foundation | Already does PPP delivery, teacher capacity-building, QAT learning assurance | "What happens between teacher training and the next QAT — and how does PEF know classroom practice changed?" |
| PECTAA | Curriculum, training and assessment remit | "How can professional development be connected to the teacher's next lesson, and then to learning evidence?" |
| Punjab P&D Board | Development planning, ADP, project approval, foreign assistance — influence on the *investment case*, not a buyer | "What evidence, conditions and cost case would be needed to support this in Punjab's development portfolio?" |
| World Bank (Punjab education / GRADES-STP) | Financier and technical influence, likely a TA/referral route rather than direct purchase | "Where could our evidence strengthen teacher-support and learning-data objectives, and which government-owned route fits?" |

Deal path on SED, seven stages, each with what happens / who decides / where it stalls / how to
maintain momentum / exit criterion. Stall points named: *sponsor moved or relationship is only
working-level* · *broad interest with no accountable owner* · *many attendees, no accountable
mover* · *jumping to an AI demo too early* · *technical support exists but no budget owner* ·
*procurement, scope creep or leadership change* · *pilot becomes the endpoint.*

His rules: keep **one executive sponsor plus one working-level champion**; build the learning
case, operating case and financial case in parallel; don't submit a full proposal until sponsor,
budget source, approval forum and procurement route are known; and —

> **"Treat interest as relationship progress — not pipeline progress — until ownership, use case
> and route are clear."**
>
> **"A signed and funded agreement... An unfunded MoU is relationship progress, not a closed
> deal."**

Plus an explicit "key assumptions to validate" list, including that he does *not* assume any
existing relationship with PEF, PECTAA or P&D, and a Research & Sources tab listing what each
public source supports.

### Why he wrote it that way

He's built the pipeline around **who can stop the deal**, not around who is friendliest. That's
why P&D and the World Bank are in the list even though neither will "buy" anything — they control
approval and financing, and a deal that clears the Department but dies at PDWP is still a dead
deal.

The two definitional lines exist because he's been burned. Nobody writes "an unfunded MoU is
relationship progress, not a closed deal" unless they've once reported an MoU as a win and then
had to explain why no money arrived.

### Why that's good — and why

**The typical answer here is five donors and a concept note** — World Bank, UNICEF, FCDO, a
foundation, another foundation, and "we'd send an introductory email." That's a fundraising list,
not a partnership pipeline, and it's exactly the confusion this role was created to end. Muneeb
doesn't make it.

- **His five are structurally different from each other.** That's the tell of someone who thinks
  in systems: a chain, not a wish-list. If PEF says no, the SED route is unaffected.
- **His opening questions are genuinely good openers**, because each one is a question *the
  official cannot answer comfortably* — "how does PEF know classroom practice changed?" is a hole
  they already suspect exists. Asking a question that makes someone want to find out the answer is
  how you get a second meeting. Compare: "we'd like to introduce our platform."
- **He names sponsor transfer as stall #1.** This is the single most telling item on the page.
  Officials move — that's the defining risk of Pakistani institutional deals — and the only people
  who build for it unprompted are people who've lost a deal that way. His answer isn't a phrase,
  either: two relationships at every institution, one senior for authority, one working-level who
  actually knows the file.
- **He refuses to inflate the pipeline.** Both quoted lines above are him *taking away* his own
  ability to report good news. That's rare and it's exactly what you want in someone whose
  forecasts leadership will plan around.
- **His 30-day "progress" definitions are honest.** "Executive sponsor and technical owner
  identified; one use case; scoping meeting" — not "MoU signed." Government does not move in 30
  days, and a candidate who promises five signed agreements in a month has never watched an
  approval file travel.
- **He labels what he doesn't know.** Explicitly assumes no existing relationship with three of
  the five. The brief asked for labelled assumptions; most people label none.

**Soft spot:** every route is researched, none is warm. He doesn't claim a personal relationship
with any of the five — which is honest, but it means we don't yet know whether he can *open* a
door or only map one. That's Section 6's job.

### Questions to ask

1. "Of these five, which one could you personally get a meeting with this month — and through
   whom?" — *"None, but here's how I'd build the route" is a perfectly good answer given honestly.
   A vague claim of contacts is much worse.*
2. "You listed 'sponsor moved' as the first stall. Tell me about a time that actually happened to
   you." — *The best question in this document. A real story arrives instantly, with names.*
3. "Your sponsor transfers out three months in. It's Monday morning. What do you do first?" —
   *Tests plan versus phrase.*
4. "You wrote that an unfunded MoU isn't a closed deal. Have you ever had to tell a boss that?" —
   *Goes straight at commercial honesty under pressure — and at whether he'll tell* us *bad news.*
5. "PEF says yes, then goes quiet for six weeks. Walk me through week seven." — *Sets up Section 7.*

## 6. Assignment 4 — Extension or Renewal (the money one)

### What he wrote

**Two tracks in parallel:** secure a short, affordable *extension* inside the existing project
envelope to protect continuity, while immediately starting a **3-year renewal** case. Permanent
recurring funding is the long-term destination, but only if government is prepared to absorb
coaching as a permanent function.

**Day one is the money, not the strategy.** His first move is not "extension or renewal" — it's
whether there is a *legally usable* balance. He wants the approved PC-I and revisions, release
history, budget-versus-actual by head, committed procurements, unpaid liabilities, staff
contracts, the monthly burn rate, and confirmation of which heads can legally support an
extension. Then he models 3-, 6- and 9-month bridge options. His decision rule: *the longest
bridge the Finance-certified, uncommitted, legally usable balance can safely support — without
assuming an accounting balance is automatically available.*

**Rules literacy:** he cites that project-funded development posts cease when a project closes,
and that converting essential posts to current expenditure needs a separate approval process —
which is *why* repeated project-cycle dependence is structurally fragile, and why he'd pursue a
3-year renewal with annual evidence gates rather than promise regularisation.

**Moving the file:** seven stages, each with what he needs / what he does / the exit condition —
NIETE Finance → NIETE leadership → MoFEPT pre-brief → formal file → Planning/Finance →
contract action → renewal in parallel. Crucially, **Taleemabad builds the model and Finance
certifies it**, and he prepares the *decision pack, revised work plan, staffing plan and
forwarding note for NIETE to issue.*

His operating principle, verbatim: **"I would never leave a meeting without an owner and a date,
and I would never leave a file at an office without knowing what must happen for it to move."**

**Access tactics:** use the PA / office focal person as an operational relationship but never
treat the PA as the decision-maker; ask for ten focused minutes and send the one-pager first;
make every visit about one decision or one blocker, never "checking the file"; send the exact
document requested after every meeting; keep two relationships at every institution; use
introductions from NIETE and Ministry counterparts rather than repeated cold visits.

**The money case:** 60 coaches, 3 coach managers, 1 programme manager, 1 finance, 1 admin —
PKR 11.85m/month salaries + PKR 2.95m/month operating = **PKR 14.8m/month**. Three-year view with
**8% annual escalation**: 177.6m → 191.8m → 207.2m, **total PKR 576.56m**. Every line marked
"replace with Finance-certified data."

**Negotiation posture per table:** Finance tests accuracy and eligibility · MoFEPT tests public
value and continuity · Planning Commission tests sustainability and outcomes — with a different
pitch for each.

### Why that's good — and why

Measured against the extension brief's own evaluator guide, he hits four of the five markers hard:

- **Full-chain ownership — the make-or-break test.** The brief's guide says anyone who says "the
  Ministry will take it forward" or "the PC-1 is their department's job" has *failed the core
  test*. Muneeb does the opposite: he builds the financial model for Finance to certify, and he
  **drafts the forwarding note for NIETE to issue**. Writing the government's own letter for them
  is precisely the behaviour the case was built to find.
- **Costing realism.** The guide warns that a flat budget carried forward means they can't do the
  job. His model escalates 8% a year on both salaries and operations. He passes cleanly.
- **Relationship craft.** The guide asks for concrete human tactics — the PA who tips you off —
  not abstract "stakeholder engagement." He names the PA relationship explicitly *and* the trap
  ("never treat the PA as the decision-maker"). Direct hit.
- **Rules literacy.** The guide wants awareness that allocations are governed by clauses you must
  learn proactively. He's citing PC-I, PDWP, ADP, foreign-assistance routes, and the
  development-post-versus-current-expenditure distinction. Well past the bar.

**And "start with the money, not the strategy" is the mark of someone who has done this.** The
instinct of almost everyone is to argue *why the programme deserves to continue*. His instinct is
to find out *what can legally be spent* — because a beautiful case for a bridge you cannot fund
wastes three months and 60 people's jobs.

### ⚠️ The one place he goes against our own evaluator guide — and this is your best discussion

The guide's marker #3 is **negotiation buffer**: a strong answer *pitches above true requirement*,
knowing three successive rounds of cuts stand between the ask and the allocation (Finance
negotiates first, Ministry second, Planning Commission third), and can defend where the buffer
sits.

**Muneeb explicitly refuses to do this.** In his own words: the formal request should be *"the
verified cost of the minimum capability plus evidence-led programme delivery,"* and *"if a
contingency is requested, itemise it rather than padding the number."*

Do not treat this as a wrong answer. It is a **considered, principled position**, and our scoring
rule is to reward reasoning rather than agreement. His logic is coherent: pad the number and you
lose credibility with Finance, which is the one table where credibility is the only currency you
have.

But it carries a real risk, and he doesn't acknowledge it: **if you ask for the true minimum and
then get cut three times, you end up funded below viability** — and you cannot go back for more.
The strongest possible answer holds both: ask defensibly, but structure the ask so that what gets
cut is severable (itemised contingency, phased scope, flexible costs before core coaching — which,
notably, he *does* say to Finance) rather than cutting into the coaching itself.

**This is the single best conversation you can have with him in the debrief.** It's a genuine
tension, he's clearly thought about it, and how he handles being pushed on it tells you more than
any question about his CV.

### One accuracy slip worth naming — the 60 coaches

He writes that *"the case-study provides assumptions for 60 coaches and indicative salaries,"* and
labels every line of the money model "Case-study assumption."

**The brief contains no figures at all.** Ayesha's editorial rule for it was explicitly no real
budget numbers in candidate-facing text — the pack only says the contracted team's costs include
salaries, operational and logistics costs. So **60 coaches at PKR 170,000 is his own assumption,
attributed to us.**

Keep this proportionate. It is **not fabrication**: he says outright "It is not a claim about
NIETE's actual payroll," marks every line "replace with Finance-certified data," and titles the
section "Illustrative Model." Inventing a working number to build a model against a brief that
withheld one is exactly the right instinct — the brief was *testing* whether he'd build a costing
at all.

But he sourced his own assumption to the document, in the one section where he was making numbers
up. It's a small slip and an easy habit to correct. It matters because his whole submission's
strength is evidence discipline, and this is the one place it wobbles — so ask him, and watch
whether he owns it cleanly or explains it away.

### Questions to ask

1. "You said don't pad the number. Finance cuts 15%, the Ministry cuts 10%, and the Planning
   Commission cuts another 20%. You're now funded below what the programme needs. What did you do
   wrong?" — **Ask this one.** *Best question in the pack. Looking for: he holds his position with
   a better structure (severable contingency, phased scope) rather than either collapsing or
   digging in.*
2. "Where did 60 coaches at PKR 170,000 come from? I don't think that was in the brief." — *Ask it
   neutrally. Looking for a clean "that was my assumption, I should have labelled it as mine"
   rather than a defence.*
3. "NIETE Finance won't certify a balance — they keep saying they'll get to it. Three weeks gone,
   coaches' contracts end in six. What do you do?" — *Tests whether full-chain ownership survives
   contact with a passive counterpart.*
4. "You'd draft the forwarding note for NIETE to issue. Has an institution ever let you do that?
   How did you get to that level of trust?" — *Verifies the strongest claim in his submission.*
5. "Which is worse: the programme pauses for two months, or 60 coaches sign contracts you're not
   certain you can fund past March?" — *A real ethical fork. There's no clean answer; you're

## 8. Live scenarios to run in the room

The paper is excellent and AI-assisted-and-disclosed. That combination means **the live scenarios
are where you actually learn who he is.** Run them cold, don't send them in advance.

How to run each one: read the setup out loud, let him answer, then **push back once**. The

### Scenario A — "There is no budget" (the one you asked for)

> *You're in the Ministry. You've made the case for the extension. The official hears you out and
> says: "Look, I agree with everything you've said. But there is no budget. Whatever you want to
> do, do it inside what you already have."*
>
> *You know the envelope doesn't stretch. What do you say — in that meeting, not afterwards?*

**Why this scenario:** it's not hypothetical. The extension brief states that the Ministry's
default position *will* be "no additional budget — fit it inside what you already have." This is
the actual conversation.

**What a strong answer does.** In roughly this order:

1. **Accepts the constraint out loud instead of arguing with it.** "Understood — no new money."
   Arguing with a budget statement makes the official defend it, and now they're committed.
2. **Separates the two questions hiding inside "no budget."** *No budget this year* and *no budget
   ever* are completely different problems, and officials use the same sentence for both. A strong
   candidate finds out which one he's in — usually by asking about the cycle: when does the next
   ADP window open, when is the PC-I intake, when does the file need to exist to make that window.
3. **Changes the question from money to continuity.** "What's the minimum that keeps the coaching
   capability alive, and can it be funded from the existing envelope?" This is a smaller, cheaper
   thing to say yes to — and it's a question the official can actually answer.
4. **Asks for something that costs nothing.** A nomination of a focal person. A letter of support.
   Permission to work with Finance on the numbers. A date. These preserve momentum without needing
   a rupee, and officials give them freely because they carry no financial risk.
5. **Looks for whose budget it could be instead.** Existing programme funds, ADP, a foreign-
   assisted project, reallocation between heads, a development partner already funding something
   adjacent. He wrote all four of these routes in Assignment 4 — check he can say them out loud
   under pressure.
6. **Names what stops if nothing happens — factually, once, without pleading.** 60 coaches'
   contracts end, this many teachers lose support, and here is what that costs the Ministry in the
   evidence they were about to publish. Then stops talking.
7. **Leaves with an owner and a date.** His own stated principle. Hold him to it.

**What a weak answer does — and why each one is bad:**

| Weak move | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Repeats the case louder, with more impact statistics | The official already agreed with the case. He's not unconvinced, he's unfunded. Re-arguing tells him you weren't listening, which is the fastest way to lose the relationship. |
| "We can do it free / at cost for a few months" | Destroys the price permanently, sets the precedent that our work is discretionary, and — worse — commits Taleemabad's money in a room where he has no authority to do that. Watch for this one; it's the most common instinct and it's a serious flag. |
| Goes above the official to the Secretary | Might work once. Ends the working relationship with the person who actually moves files, and files move at working level. |
| Uses a donor or a deadline as pressure | "Our funder needs an answer by month-end" makes their problem your problem's hostage. Officials remember being squeezed. |
| "I'll follow up next week" with nothing specific | This is how a live conversation becomes a dead one. No owner, no date, no question — nothing to follow up *on*. |
| Accepts it and leaves | Polite, and fatal. The meeting ends with the file exactly where it was and no reason for anyone to open it again. |

**What to listen for, mechanically:**

- **Does he ask a question in the first thirty seconds?** Strong operators respond to "no" with a
  question, because "no budget" is information they need to unpack, not a verdict to accept or
  fight. Weak ones respond with a statement.
- **Does the ask get smaller?** The single best indicator. Practitioners shrink the ask until it
  fits what the person can actually approve. Pitchers keep the ask the same size and just push
  harder.
- **Does he stay inside the official's authority?** A good answer asks the official for things
  that are *his to give.* A bad one asks for things that require someone else — which is really
  asking him to expose himself upward on your behalf.
- **Does he protect the person?** Government officials say no to protect themselves. If Muneeb
  gives him a version he can say yes to without risk, that's the whole skill.

## 9. The decoder: alive, dying, dead, or silent

You asked how to tell whether he's keeping a conversation alive, killing it, or has gone quiet.
Two uses for this: **judging his answers** in the scenarios above, and **judging him later** if he
joins.

### Reading the conversation

| State | What it looks like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Alive** | They ask *you* for something. They name another person. They give a date. They raise an objection. They forward your document internally. They ask what it costs. | Progress. **Objections are the strongest alive signal of all** — someone who argues with your plan is imagining doing it. Silence is not agreement; disagreement is engagement. |
| **Warm but stalled** | Friendly meetings, no new names, no dates. "Send us your proposal and we'll review it." "Let me discuss internally." Praise with no action. | The relationship is real and the deal is not. Needs a smaller ask, or a different person. Danger: this state feels like progress for months. |
| **Dying** | Meetings rescheduled without a reason. Replies get shorter. You're handed down to someone junior with no brief. Your questions go unanswered while pleasantries continue. | Something changed internally and nobody will tell you. Stop presenting; start diagnosing. |
| **Dead** | The file comes back. Nobody claims ownership. "Not a priority this financial year" with no alternative route. Your sponsor is gone and no one picks it up. | Ask for a clean no and a reason. A documented no is worth more than a warm maybe — it tells you what to change and it frees the effort. |

### Reading silence — there are three kinds, and they need different responses

Most people treat all silence the same way (send another follow-up). A strong candidate
distinguishes:

1. **Busy silence.** Nothing changed; you're just not urgent. *Response:* make it easy and cheap.
   One short message, one specific question answerable in a line, no attachments. Or go via the
   office focal person — the PA relationship he already flagged.
2. **Blocked silence.** Someone internally objects, and your contact won't be the one to tell you.
   *Response:* stop asking your contact for a decision and start asking for *information* — "is
   there a concern I should be addressing?" Or get the read from a different relationship at the
   same institution. This is precisely what his two-relationships rule is for.
3. **No-they-won't-say silence.** The answer is no; the culture makes saying it awkward. *Response:*
   make it graceful to say. "If this isn't the right year, that's genuinely useful to know — is
   there a better window, or a different route I should be looking at?" You get the truth *and*
   keep the relationship for next time.

**A candidate who names all three is telling you they've lived through all three.**

### What kills a conversation — the things to listen for him doing

- Repeating the same ask, louder or more often. Volume never converts a no.
- Following up with nothing new in the message. "Just checking in" is a withdrawal from the
  relationship account with no deposit.
- Using pressure — a deadline, a competitor, a donor, an impact statistic deployed as guilt.
- Cutting price or offering free work to keep momentum. Buys weeks, costs the value permanently.
- Escalating over someone's head without telling them.
- Asking for a decision the person doesn't have the authority to make. It forces a no.
- Going quiet himself because he has no news. Silence from our side reads as loss of interest, and
  officials rarely restart a conversation.

### What keeps it alive

- Return with something useful, not a reminder — an answer, an analysis, a precedent, an
  introduction.
- Shrink the ask until it fits what this person can approve alone.
- Ask for a person or a date rather than a decision.
- Give them something to react to; a draft gets edits, a question gets silence.
- Two relationships at every institution, always.
- Ask directly whether there's a concern — and mean it.
- Make the no easy. It's the fastest route to either the truth or the real objection.

**Scoring note for the room:** in every scenario above, ask yourself one question — *did his move
make it easier or harder for the other person to keep talking to him?* That single test predicts
performance in this role better than any answer about strategy.
