## 2. How to read this document (no jargon)

For each assignment: **what he wrote**, in plain English → **why he wrote it that way** → **why

## 3. Assignment 1 — The one-pager and the deck

### What he wrote

Title: *Strengthening Learning Outcomes in Punjab* — addressed to the **School Education
Department, Punjab.**

His argument: Punjab has built one of Pakistan's strongest education *delivery and governance*
systems, access has improved, but **learning outcomes haven't followed** — and closing that gap
needs classroom instruction, teacher support and evidence-based decisions rather than more digital
tools. Taleemabad has "evolved from an educational content platform into a government
implementation partner" that plugs into existing structures rather than creating parallel ones.

Then: a *Why Taleemabad?* section listing eight capabilities; a *Proven Impact* section with
figures; a *Value for the Punjab government* section (better teaching, improved learning, smarter
decisions, sustainable capacity); and a three-phase approach — **Joint Diagnostic (4–6 weeks) →
Demonstration → Scale.**

The deck: title slide plus five narrative slides — *Punjab's Next Education Challenge* → *Who We
Are: Taleemabad's Journey* → *The Integrated Learning Model* → *Evidence That It Works* → *A
Partnership Roadmap for Punjab.* Each carries a "why this slide" line, exactly as the brief asked.

### Why he wrote it that way

He decided credibility had to come before the ask, and he says so in his own slide rationale — the
opening slide "acknowledges Punjab's achievements before introducing the challenge, building
credibility and avoiding the impression of criticizing the system," and the *Who We Are* slide
exists to "reassure the Secretary that Taleemabad is not another edtech vendor, but a long-term
implementation partner."

**That's the single most encouraging thing in the whole submission.** He isn't accidentally getting
the register right — he can articulate *why* it's right. That's a transferable skill, not a lucky
draft.

### Why the good parts are good

Compare to what most people write. **The standard version opens with Pakistan's learning crisis** —
out-of-school children, learning poverty, national tragedy — then presents us as the answer. It
reads well to us and lands badly on a Secretary, because he is the person accountable for that
crisis and you've just explained his own job back to him.

Waqas never does this. He credits Punjab's investment first, then frames the gap as
**access-without-learning** — which is a real, well-understood policy problem the Secretary can
discuss without losing face. Three more genuine strengths:

- **"Technology is designed to strengthen teachers not replace them,"** stated plainly. Teacher
  displacement is the first objection any education bureaucrat raises. He kills it early.
- **"Rather than creating parallel systems, Taleemabad integrates... into existing government
  structures."** Officials have watched a decade of programmes that ran alongside the system and
  vanished with their funding. Saying you'll work inside their structures is the reassurance that
  matters most.
- **Phase 1 is a 4–6 week joint diagnostic, not a pilot.** Small, cheap, reversible, and it makes
  government a co-author of the diagnosis. People defend what they helped write.

### Where it weakens — three specific things

**1. There is no decision requested.** The one-pager ends on an aspiration: *"Taleemabad seeks to
strengthen not replace the education system Punjab has already built."* That's a nice sentence and
the Secretary cannot act on it. A policy one-pager should end with one question he can answer in the
meeting — which priority, which unit, which district, who leads. Without that, the most likely
outcome of a good briefing is a warm handshake and no file.

**2. The Proposed Partnership Approach is three empty headings.** In the one-pager, "Phase 1 –
Joint Diagnostic (4–6 Weeks) / Phase 2 – Demonstration / Phase 3 – Scale" appear with **no content
underneath them.** The deck gives each one line. So the section a Secretary would actually read
twice — what does this look like, what would we be committing to — is the thinnest part of the
document. That reads like time running out, which is forgivable, but it's the wrong section to run
out of time on.

**3. "Why Taleemabad?" drifts into vendor register.** Eight capability bullets, then a stat wall.
That's a product page. The version that works reframes capability as *evidence of scar tissue* —
we ran 140 low-fee schools ourselves, so we know what actually breaks in a classroom. He has that
material (the deck's *School Implementation* slide says "gaining deep insights into classroom
realities") but doesn't use it as the argument.

**Minor polish:** his opening two paragraphs say nearly the same sentence twice ("Punjab has
established one of Pakistan's most mature public education delivery systems" / "one of Pakistan's
strongest education management systems"). And the one-pager runs to roughly 1,100 words — closer to
a five-minute read than the three minutes the brief specified.

### ⚠️ Figures to verify before the debrief

He states as **"Proven Impact"**: 8.5 million children reached weekly through educational TV and
radio · 1.5 million+ registered learners · **1.5 additional Learning Adjusted Years of Schooling
(LAYS) reported through independent evaluations** · 77% of teachers reported improved productivity ·
87% of parents reported stronger learning engagement.

**Only the 1.5 million figure appears in the brief.** The other four don't. Two possibilities:

- **They're our real published figures** → good research, and this is fine. The brief permitted
  publicly verifiable information.
- **Any of them is invented** → serious, and made worse by presentation. He labels the section
  "Proven Impact" and gives no source and no hedge. He was careful to hedge in Assignment 4
  ("illustrative assumptions") — so he knows how to caveat, and chose not to here.

**Please have whoever owns our impact numbers check these.** It's a ten-minute check and it moves
the verdict either way.

### Also worth resolving: why Punjab?

The brief says the Secretary is from **"a province where it does not yet work."** Waqas wrote to
Punjab — and then made Punjab the subject of Assignment 3 as well. If Punjab counts as a
not-yet province, the choice is fine and the reuse is efficient. If we already work there, then
Assignment 1's entire premise is off. You'll know which; I don't, so I'm not scoring it either way.

### Questions to ask

1. "Pitch me the one-pager. I'm the Secretary, you have three minutes." — *Listen for whether he
   leads with the access-vs-learning argument or the capability list.*
2. "You end by saying Taleemabad seeks to strengthen the system. If I say 'yes, agreed' — what have
   I actually agreed to, and what do you want from me before I leave the room?" — *Goes straight at
   the missing ask. A strong answer produces the specific decision on the spot.*
3. "Your Phase 1 is a 4–6 week joint diagnostic. Walk me through it — who's in it, what do they do,
   what do I get at the end?" — *Fills the empty section, live. This is where you find out whether
   the phases were a plan or a placeholder.*
4. "Where did 8.5 million weekly and the 1.5 LAYS figure come from?" — *Ask it neutrally. You want
   a source, or a clean "that was from your public material and I should have cited it."*
5. "Why did you write this for Punjab?" — *Opens the Karachi-versus-Lahore question gently, before
   Scenario G makes it explicit.*

## 4. Assignment 2 — The Room

### What he wrote

*Accelerating System-Wide Learning Improvements in Pakistan.* Theme: **Evidence. Partnerships.
Scale.** A **half-day executive forum, ~4 hours, 60–80 senior participants.**

His framing is right at the top: *"The objective of this convening is not to celebrate Taleemabad's
impact, but to establish Taleemabad as a trusted thought partner"* — shifting the room from *"What
has Taleemabad achieved?"* to *"How can governments and partners work together to improve learning
outcomes at scale?"*

**The morning-after test, quantified by group:** three-plus provincial or federal departments
express interest · two Secretaries agree to technical follow-up · we're seen as an implementation
partner, not another edtech provider · two development organisations request technical
presentations or concept notes · and internally, within seven days, *every strategic conversation
has an owner* and a pipeline exists.

Ten attendee types, each with a desired takeaway: provincial Secretaries, MoFEPT / National
Curriculum Council, PMIU and provincial reform units, teacher-training institutions (QAED),
development partners (UNICEF, World Bank, FCDO, GPE, UNESCO, ADB), education foundations (PEF),
think tanks (IDEAS, CDPR, Pakistan Institute of Education), university researchers, philanthropic
and CSR funders, and selected education media.

Agenda: CEO welcome (10 min) → keynote by a senior policymaker (20) → research launch (25) →
fireside with government, a development partner and our CEO (30) → networking break (20, with
leadership assigned to pre-identified stakeholders) → **interactive policy roundtables "instead of
speeches"** on four themes, each facilitated by Taleemabad, each producing three recommendations →
closing (15), ending on *"Today's discussion begins a partnership not concludes an event."*

Talking points: *Technology Does Not Improve Learning; Teachers Do* · *The Future of Education Is
System Strengthening* · *Evidence Should Drive Scale.*

Follow-up: within 24 hours, personalised CEO thank-yous, the report, an executive summary, photos,
the presentation link. Within 7 days, differentiated by stakeholder — governments get a policy
brief aligned to *their* provincial priorities plus a workshop request and a meeting proposal;
development partners get a concept note and an evidence package; researchers get the full technical
report; media get a press release. Then a **pipeline conversion table** with a success indicator per
stakeholder type, and success measured at 7 days / 30–90 days / 6–12 months.

### Why he wrote it that way

He's designed this as a **relationship-generation event**: get the right seniority in one room,
give them a credible reason to be there, make sure every conversation is owned afterwards. The
follow-up matrix is the most developed part of the whole submission, which tells you his instinct
is right about where the value is.

### Why the good parts are good

- **The quantified morning-after is genuinely strong and rarer than it sounds.** "Two Secretaries
  agree to technical follow-up meetings" is a target you can pass or fail. Compare the usual
  version — "stakeholders leave with a positive impression of Taleemabad" — which can never be
  measured and therefore never learned from.
- **"Every strategic conversation has an owner" within seven days.** Unowned follow-up is how good
  events produce nothing, and he closed that hole deliberately.
- **The follow-up is differentiated, not blanket.** Governments get their *own* provincial
  priorities mapped; researchers get methodology; funders get an evidence package. He understands
  these people want different things — the actual skill of convening.
- **Roundtables instead of speeches, with a required output.** Three recommendations per table
  forces participation and gives him a legitimate reason to make contact afterwards ("you raised
  X — here's the follow-up on it").
- **"Technology Does Not Improve Learning; Teachers Do"** is the strongest line in the submission.
  For a technology organisation to lead with that in front of government is disarming and true.

### Where it weakens — and why it matters

**1. It's a forum, so nobody can challenge us.** Every roundtable is facilitated by Taleemabad. The
fireside is a government rep, a development partner and our own CEO. There is no independent voice
whose job is to interrogate the study, and **nowhere does he state what the evidence does *not*
show.** That's the gap that matters most, because "thought leadership" among senior technical
people is not built by presenting well — it's built by surviving scrutiny. A room of researchers
and Secretaries will privately ask "how big was the sample, how were schools selected, who paid for
the evaluation," and if we haven't raised it, they'll conclude we hoped they wouldn't.

**2. Podium time is still about a third of the event.** CEO welcome, keynote and research launch
are 55 minutes before the first real dialogue. His own instinct — "instead of speeches" — is
correct, and the agenda doesn't fully carry it through.

**3. The success metrics contradict his opening claim.** He opens with "success will not be measured
by attendance or media coverage alone," and then measures **70% attendance** and lists a press
release, key findings and expert interviews for media. Those are launch-event metrics. Not fatal —
media coverage is a legitimate secondary aim — but it shows the launch-event template asserting
itself over the working-session intent he stated.

**4. 60–80 seniors in four hours is a lot of competing agendas**, and he never says which two
attendees actually matter most. Several "desired takeaways" are near-duplicates of each other
("this is a credible partner" / "this is a scalable implementation model" / "this complements our
systems"), which is a sign the room was built by category rather than by decision.

### Questions to ask

1. "A Secretary in the room says the sample is too small to justify provincial scale. It's a fair
   point, and forty senior people are listening. What do you say?" — *The most important question
   in this section, because his design has no mechanism for it. Watch for concede-and-reframe
   versus defend-or-deflect.*
2. "You've got 60–80 people. Budget halves, the room is 15. Who stays?" — *Forces the
   prioritisation the brief avoids.*
3. "Would you put someone in that room who doesn't rate us — a rival provider, a sceptical
   researcher? Why or why not?" — *Tests whether inviting scrutiny is available to him as an idea
   at all.*
4. "You said success isn't attendance or coverage, then measured 70% attendance and a press
   release. Which is it?" — *Not a trap; a real tension. You're testing whether he can notice it
   and choose.*
5. "It's day 3 after the event. Who are you sitting with, and what are you asking them for?" —
   *Follow-up plans written to fill a section collapse here. His probably won't; the matrix is
   real.*

## 5. Assignment 3 — The Pipeline

### What he wrote

Thesis: as Taleemabad expands into Punjab, prioritise partnerships offering **Scale, System
Strengthening, and Sustainability**, and spread across institution types to reduce "dependence on
any single stakeholder."

Five partners:

| # | Partner | His logic | His opening line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **School Education Department, Punjab** | System owner; 48,000+ schools | "Punjab has built one of Pakistan's strongest education systems…" |
| 2 | **PMIU (SED Punjab)** | Technical implementation and monitoring arm | "PMIU has transformed education monitoring in Punjab…" |
| 3 | **QAED** | Punjab's teacher professional development institution | "How can QAED and Taleemabad work together to…" |
| 4 | **The Citizens Foundation** | Large not-for-profit network; classroom validation and innovation pilots | "Both organizations share a commitment to…" |
| 5 | **MoFEPT** | Federal policy, curriculum, national replication — marked **"Existing Relationship"** | "Building on our previous collaboration…" |

A prioritisation matrix scoring each on Policy Influence / Scale / Ease of Engagement / Strategic
Alignment, footnoted honestly as "the author's qualitative ranking."

An 8-stage SED deal path: **Understand Before You Pitch** → Executive Engagement → Technical
Validation → Co-Design → Internal Government Approvals (chain named: Secretary SED → PMIU →
Finance → Legal/Procurement) → Partnership Agreement (signed MoU + quarterly Joint Steering
Committee) → Implementation (first 90 days) → Scale & Institutionalisation.

A tracker with engagement stage, next action, **owner** (Growth Manager; +Product Team; +CEO) and a
30-day success measure per partner. A stakeholder journey running all the way down to district
authorities, school heads and teachers. A risks table: leadership changes → build relationships
across multiple levels · budget constraints → position as strengthening existing investments, phase
implementation · **perception as an EdTech vendor** → lead with evidence and teacher development ·
slow processes → ready-to-use documentation · competing priorities → align with Punjab's existing
reform agenda. And a 30-60-90 plan.

### Why the good parts are good

- **"Understand Before You Pitch" as stage one.** Reviewing the sector plan, PMIU's systems and
  QAED's framework *before* the first meeting is exactly right, and it's the stage most candidates
  skip entirely because it produces nothing to show.
- **The approval chain is named.** Secretary → PMIU → Finance → Legal/Procurement is where deals
  actually die, and most submissions treat government approval as a single box called "approval."
- **The tracker has owners and 30-day measures.** It's a usable document, not a formatted table
  with nothing to enter — and the 30-day measures are appropriately modest ("agreement to hold a
  technical workshop," "technical validation meeting completed"), not signed contracts.
- **"Perception as an EdTech vendor" listed as a business risk.** That's self-awareness about how
  we're received, and the mitigation (lead with teacher development and evidence, not product) is
  the right one.
- **He picked institutions that do genuinely different jobs** — policy owner, technical/monitoring
  arm, teacher-development body, delivery network, federal policy. If QAED says no, the SED route
  is unaffected.

### Where it weakens — the three that matter

**1. No development-sector partner.** The brief asked for five "across government and the
development sector." His five are four government/quasi-government bodies plus one NGO. The
development partners (World Bank, UNICEF, FCDO, ADB) appear in his *convening* room but never in
his *pipeline* — so he knows they exist and didn't treat them as partnership targets. That's an
instruction miss, and it also matters substantively: in Pakistani education, the financier is often
the one who decides whether a government programme happens at all.

**2. A signed MoU is treated as the win.** Stage 6's success indicator is "Signed MoU; Steering
Committee established," and the 90-day plan ends at "MoU finalization... formal partnership
established." **An MoU with no funding attached is a piece of paper**, and forecasting one inside 90
days through a chain that includes Finance, Legal and Procurement is optimistic to the point of
being a planning risk. What's missing is the distinction between *relationship progress* and
*pipeline progress* — the discipline that stops leadership planning against deals that haven't
happened.

**3. Stalls are generic, not stage-specific.** The brief asked where the deal will **probably
stall.** He has a risk table — leadership change, budget, slow processes — which is a category list
that would fit any deal in any sector. What's absent is the specific institutional failure mode:
which *office* holds it, whether the money exists at all, what happens at the budget-cycle boundary,
and the fact that technical support and budget authority usually sit with different people.

**Two smaller things worth raising:**

- **SED scores 5/5 for "Ease of Engagement."** The largest education department in the country,
  managing 48,000+ schools, is never the easiest partner to engage. Either the matrix is scoring
  *strategic desirability* under an "ease" label, or he genuinely believes it — and both are worth
  a question.
- **TCF is his current employer, and he doesn't say so.** He's been at The Citizens Foundation for
  three years. Listing TCF as partner #3 with an owner of "Growth Manager + CEO" is arguably
  shrewd — it may be the one genuinely warm route on the page. But the submission never discloses
  the relationship. A candidate who says "I currently work here, so I know this route and here's
  the conflict to manage" is much stronger than one who leaves us to notice. Ask him.

### Questions to ask

1. "The brief asked for government and the development sector. All five of yours are government or
   an NGO. Talk me through that." — *Looking for a real thesis — e.g. "donors don't buy, government
   does" — rather than an oversight admission.*
2. "You've got TCF at number three. You work there. How does that conversation actually go, and
   what's the conflict?" — **Ask this one.** *Best integrity read available in his pack.*
3. "You've signed the MoU at day 90. Is that a closed deal?" — *You want him to say no, and to say
   what's missing: money, a budget owner, a start date.*
4. "Where will the SED deal most likely die, specifically — which office, which month?" —
   *Pushes past the generic risk table. The strongest answer names the gap between technical
   support and budget authority.*
5. "You rated SED five out of five on ease of engagement. Really?" — *Asked lightly. You're testing
   whether he'll revise a judgment when challenged, which is also a values read.*

## 6. Assignment 4 — Extension or Renewal (the money one)

### What he wrote

A **sequenced strategy**, and he argues for it rather than just asserting it: an extension alone
"only postpones the problem," while a renewal alone "risks disrupting the programme, losing trained
coaches, and weakening government confidence." So: **Phase 1** — a 6–12 month extension inside the
existing approved envelope to prevent interruption; **Phase 2** — simultaneously start a 3–5 year
renewal through a new PC-1 aiming at annual institutional funding.

**Day one is a situation assessment before approaching anyone**: remaining budget, salary
commitments, OPEX, logistics, contractual obligations, utilisation to date; then programme
performance (teachers supported, sessions delivered, outcomes, KPIs); then administrative status
(approval documents, funding conditions, end date, reporting obligations). Output: an internal
assessment report covering remaining funds, extension feasibility, renewal requirements, key risks.

**The business case answers three questions from the government's point of view** — why should this
continue, why now, why Taleemabad.

**Stakeholder journey, five stages:** internal alignment (one agreed position before touching
government) → **host institute first**, because it's "the programme owner and the strongest advocate
within government" → the institute's Finance Department → MoFEPT (Secretary, Joint Secretary,
Project Director) → Planning Commission, with the five questions he expects there (why continue
funding, what evidence, how does it align with national priorities, what happens if funding stops,
why is this value for money).

**How he moves the file**, verbatim: *"Government approvals rarely move because a document is
submitted—they move because someone consistently follows through."* His tactics: map every approval
stage and the officer responsible; prepare **decision-ready documentation to reduce the workload on
government counterparts**; systematic post-meeting action notes; relationships with **section
officers and technical staff who handle day-to-day processing**, not only leadership; request
introductions from champions inside the host institute when access is limited; scheduled review
meetings rather than email; anticipate questions and prepare documents before they're asked. And:
*"My role is to become the coordinator who keeps momentum across institutions rather than waiting
for each office to act independently"* — closing on ensuring "no file stalls because 'it is someone
else's job.'"

**The money case:** salaries PKR 60m → 66m → 72.6m at a stated **10% annual increment for inflation
and retention**; plus operations, logistics and travel, MEL, digital platform, and an **itemised 5%
contingency** — totalling **PKR 99.8m → 109.7m → 120.5m.** Each line carries its assumption.

**Three different pitches for three tables:** to Finance, that the budget reflects realistic
inflation and that investing now protects prior government investment and avoids the higher cost of
rebuilding later; to the Ministry, that this is not a new standalone initiative but sustaining an
existing one aligned to national priorities; to the Planning Commission, that impact is already
demonstrated and a multi-year commitment improves value for money and institutionalises coaching
"rather than repeatedly funding short-term interventions."

### Why the good parts are good

Measured against the extension brief's own evaluator guide, he clears three of the five markers:

- **Costing realism.** The guide warns that a flat budget carried forward means the candidate can't
  do the job. His escalates 10% a year, with the reason attached (inflation *and retention* — the
  retention half shows he's thinking about people leaving, not just prices rising). Clean pass.
- **Relationship craft.** The guide wants concrete access tactics, not abstract "stakeholder
  engagement." Building relationships with **section officers who handle day-to-day processing** is
  exactly the right instinct — files are moved by staff, not signed by them — and requesting
  introductions from internal champions rather than cold-calling is how access actually works.
- **Full-chain ownership, in principle.** The guide's core test is whether the candidate does the
  government's legwork themselves. His "decision-ready documentation to reduce the workload on
  government counterparts" and "no file stalls because it is someone else's job" are the right
  commitments, and the sequencing instinct — **internal alignment first, then the host institute as
  your advocate, then Finance, then the Ministry** — is correct and non-obvious. Going to the
  Ministry before the host institute has endorsed you is a classic, fatal error and he avoids it.

**And "why now" as an explicit section is smarter than it looks.** Government says no to things that
can wait. Making the cliff-edge — trained coaches lost, institutional knowledge gone — the reason
for urgency is how a continuation request gets prioritised over the twenty other files on the desk.

### Where it weakens

**1. He does not answer the negotiation question, and the brief asked it directly.** Assignment 4's
question 4 was: *"What figure do you ask for, knowing the FD negotiates first, the Ministry
negotiates second, and the Planning Commission negotiates third — and how do you justify that
figure at each table?"* He answers the second half well (three tailored pitches). He never answers
the first: no buffer, no discussion of three successive rounds of cuts, no statement of what he'd
protect if cut. His only nod is a mitigation line — "budget reductions → prioritise core coaching
functions and identify phased implementation options" — which is the right instinct buried in a
risk table rather than built into the ask. **This is the clearest gap in the submission**, and it
sits in the single most commercially consequential part of the role.

**2. Ownership is stated as principle, not mechanism.** "Decision-ready documentation" and "no file
stalls" are the right sentiments. What's missing is the specific act: *who drafts the PC-1, who
writes the institute's forwarding letter, who reconciles the Finance figures.* The difference
between believing in ownership and demonstrating it is the difference between "I would support the
approval chain" and "I would build the financial model and give it to Finance to certify, and draft
the institute's forwarding note for them to issue."

**3. Rules literacy is present but shallow.** He knows the machinery exists — PC-1, Planning
Commission, the institute's Finance Department, Secretary-level approval — because the brief told

merely sitting there, no budget-cycle timing, no distinction between project-funded posts and
permanently funded ones. That last one matters here, because it's the actual reason regularisation
is hard, and it's the difference between promising institutionalisation and understanding it.

**4. The financial model is honest but very round.** PKR 60m salaries, 12m operations, 8m

model built from "60 coaches × a salary" can be defended line by line at Finance. A model built
from round totals can't.

### Questions to ask

1. "You ask for 99.8 million. Finance cuts 15%, the Ministry 10%, the Planning Commission another
   20%. You're now funded below what the programme needs. What should you have done differently?" —
   **Ask this one.** *The gap he didn't address. Strong answer: build the ask so what gets cut is

3. "The institute's Finance Department says there's a balance, but you suspect it's already

4. "You said no file stalls because it's someone else's job. Concretely — has an institution ever

5. "Coaches' contracts expire in six weeks and no approval has moved. What do you tell the

## 7. The reflection — and why it matters more for him

**The question:** *"A room you led — a discussion, briefing, or convening where you moderated senior
stakeholders. What made it work, and what would you do differently?"* 200 words or fewer.

alternative sites and prepare a recovery plan **before** engaging the donor; then acknowledged the

clear ownership. The partnership survived.

What he'd do differently: **strengthen pre-allocation due diligence, and set up earlier
risk-communication mechanisms so stakeholders hear about uncertainty before it becomes a crisis.**

### Why this is good

- **It's a real, specific, dated situation with a cost** — six months lost, a reputational hit, a
  donor who could have walked. Not a highlight-reel story.
- **He walked into the hard conversation carrying a solution, not just an apology.** Establishing
  the facts and having alternative sites ready *before* facing the donor is the difference between
  a recovery and a confession.
- **The "differently" answer is structural, not cosmetic.** "Strengthen pre-allocation due
  diligence" is a process change that would have prevented the whole thing. Compare the usual
  answer — "I would have prepared more thoroughly" — which admits nothing and changes nothing.
- **Other people are in it.** Engineering, Legal, Programme teams as collaborators. He isn't the
  lone hero of his own story, and that is a stronger culture signal than it looks.

### Why this matters more for him than for anyone else

**His values interview was a boundary pass — 4(+) / 2(±), no margin.** One of the two ± ratings was
*Don't Walk Away from Hard Things*, and the reason was precisely this: asked when he last

specificity at the case study.**

**He passed that test here.** The land-objection story is exactly the specific, costly, owned

**The other ± is still open.** *Continuously Improve Our Craft* was ± because the only recent

### Scenario A — "There is no budget" (the one you asked for)

> *You're at the Ministry. You've made the case for the extension. The official hears you out and
> says: "I agree with everything. But there is no budget. Whatever you want to do, do it inside
> what you already have."*
>
> *You know the envelope doesn't stretch. What do you say — in the meeting, not afterwards?*

**Why this one first for him:** it's not hypothetical (the brief states the Ministry's default *will*
be "fit it inside what you already have"), and it's the exact gap in his Assignment 4 — he never
engaged the negotiation dynamic. This is his chance to show the thinking didn't make it onto paper.

**A strong answer, roughly in order:**

1. **Accepts the constraint out loud rather than arguing.** "Understood — no new money." Arguing
   makes the official defend the position, and now he's committed to it.
2. **Separates the two questions hiding in "no budget."** *No budget this year* and *no budget
   ever* are completely different problems and officials use the same sentence for both. Find out
   which by asking about the cycle — when the next PC-1 intake or budget window opens, and when the
   file must exist to make it.
3. **Changes the question from money to continuity.** "What's the minimum that keeps the coaching
   capability alive, and can that fit inside the existing envelope?" Smaller, cheaper, and something
   the official can actually answer.
4. **Asks for something that costs nothing.** A focal-person nomination, a letter of support,
   permission to work directly with the institute's Finance Department, a date. These keep the file
   moving without a rupee, and officials give them freely because they carry no financial risk.
5. **Looks for whose budget it could be instead** — reallocation between heads, an existing
   programme, the next ADP cycle, a foreign-assisted project, a development partner funding
   something adjacent.
6. **Names what stops, factually, once, without pleading.** The coaches' contracts end on this date,
   this many teachers lose support, and the evidence base the Ministry was about to publish loses
   its follow-through. Then stops talking.
7. **Leaves with an owner and a date.** He wrote that principle himself — hold him to it.

**Weak moves, and why each fails:**

| Weak move | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Re-argues the case with more impact statistics | The official already agreed. He isn't unconvinced, he's unfunded. Re-arguing signals you weren't listening. |
| "We can carry it free / at cost for a few months" | Destroys the price permanently, sets the precedent that our work is discretionary, and commits Taleemabad's money in a room where he has no authority to do it. **The most common instinct and a serious flag.** |
| Goes over the official's head to the Secretary | May work once; ends the relationship with the person who actually moves files. |
| Uses a deadline or a donor as pressure | Makes their problem hostage to ours. Officials remember being squeezed. |
| "I'll follow up next week" with nothing specific | This is precisely how a live conversation becomes a dead one — nothing to follow up *on*. |
| Accepts it politely and leaves | Fatal. The file ends where it started and nobody has a reason to reopen it. |

**What to listen for, mechanically:**

- **Does he ask a question in the first thirty seconds?** "No budget" is information to unpack, not
  a verdict to accept or fight. Practitioners respond with a question; pitchers respond with a
  statement.
- **Does the ask get smaller?** The single best indicator in the whole debrief.
- **Does he stay inside this official's authority?** Asking for something only someone else can
  approve is really asking him to expose himself upward on your behalf.
- **Does he protect the person?** Officials say no to protect themselves. Give them a version they
  can say yes to at no risk, and you have the whole skill.

## 9. The decoder: alive, dying, dead, or silent

You asked how to tell whether he's keeping a conversation alive, killing it, or has gone quiet. Use
it twice: to judge his scenario answers, and to judge him later if he joins.

### Reading the conversation

| State | What it looks like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Alive** | They ask *you* for something. They name another person. They give you a date. They raise an objection. They forward your document internally. They ask what it costs. | Progress. **An objection is the strongest alive signal there is** — someone arguing with your plan is imagining doing it. Silence is not agreement; disagreement is engagement. |
| **Warm but stalled** | Friendly meetings, no new names, no dates. "Send us the proposal and we'll review." "Let me discuss internally." Praise without action. | The relationship is real; the deal is not. Needs a smaller ask or a different person. Dangerous because it *feels* like progress for months. |
| **Dying** | Meetings rescheduled without a reason. Replies get shorter. You're handed down to someone junior with no brief. Your questions go unanswered while pleasantries continue. | Something changed internally and nobody will tell you. Stop presenting, start diagnosing. |
| **Dead** | The file comes back. Nobody claims ownership. "Not a priority this financial year" with no alternative. Your sponsor is gone and no one picks it up. | Ask for a clean no and a reason. A documented no beats a warm maybe — it tells you what to change and frees the effort. |

### Reading silence — three kinds, three different responses

Most people treat all silence the same way and send another follow-up. A strong operator
distinguishes:

1. **Busy silence.** Nothing has changed; you're just not urgent. *Response:* make it cheap — one
   short message, one specific question answerable in a line, no attachments. Or go through the
   office focal person.
2. **Blocked silence.** Someone internally objects and your contact won't be the one to tell you.
   *Response:* stop asking for a decision and start asking for information — "is there a concern I
   should be addressing?" Or get the read from a different relationship at the same institution.
   This is what a second relationship is *for*.
3. **No-they-won't-say silence.** The answer is no; saying it is socially awkward. *Response:* make
   it graceful. "If this isn't the right year, that's genuinely useful to know — is there a better
   window, or a different route?" You get the truth *and* keep the relationship for next time.

**A candidate who names all three has lived through all three.** Waqas's submission gestures at the
first — scheduled review meetings, systematic follow-up, section-officer relationships — but never
distinguishes them. See whether he can when asked.

### What kills a conversation — watch for him doing these

- Repeating the same ask, louder or more often. Volume never converts a no.
- Following up with nothing new. "Just checking in" is a withdrawal with no deposit.
- Applying pressure — a deadline, a competitor, a donor, an impact statistic used as guilt.
- Cutting price or offering free work to keep momentum. Buys weeks, costs the value permanently.
- Escalating over someone's head without telling them.
- Asking for a decision the person lacks authority to make. It forces a no.
- Going quiet himself because he has no news. Silence from our side reads as lost interest, and
  officials rarely restart a conversation.

### What keeps it alive

- Return with something useful, not a reminder — an answer, an analysis, a precedent, an
  introduction.
- Shrink the ask until it fits what this person can approve alone.
- Ask for a person or a date rather than a decision.
- Give them something to react to. A draft gets edits; a question gets silence.
- Two relationships at every institution, always — one senior for authority, one working-level who
  knows the file.
- Ask directly whether there's a concern, and mean it.
- Make the no easy. Fastest route to either the truth or the real objection.

**One test to carry into every scenario:** *did his move make it easier or harder for the other
person to keep talking to him?* That predicts performance in this role better than any answer about
strategy.
