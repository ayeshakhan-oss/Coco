# GM "The Story, the Room, and the Deal" — Interview Debrief Guide

**Role:** Growth Manager · **Case study:** [gm_case_study_story_room_deal.md](../gm_case_study_story_room_deal.md)
**Built:** 2026-08-17 · **Audience:** the debrief panel, People & Culture co-interviewer in particular
**Status:** DRAFT — pending Ayesha's QA. No candidate had been read when this was written.

---

## How to read this document

This is not an answer key with one right answer. This case study has no dataset and no
arithmetic — three of its four parts are judgment, register and relationship craft, which means
**there is no correct answer to check against, only a standard of reasoning to hold.**

So the document is built for the question you actually asked: *why did this person answer it this
way, and what do I ask next?* Each assignment gets four things:

1. **What the question was built to expose** — the muscle, and the trap.
2. **The answer patterns you will see** — the four or five shapes submissions actually take, and
   what each one tells you about how the person works. This is the "why did they write this"
   section.
3. **Strong / median / weak markers** — what to underline in the document.
4. **Probes** — questions to ask in the room, written to be said out loud.

Two cross-cutting sections sit at the front and back: the **register read** (the single most
useful P&C signal in this whole case) and the **traps table**.

**A submission does not have to reach our conclusions to score well.** A candidate who picks a
different province, a different room, a different lead partner and defends it with a real
argument scores as high as one who picks ours.

---

## The register read — the most useful P&C signal in this case

Everything in this case study is written *to* someone: a Secretary, a room of 8–12 stakeholder
types, a prospective partner, us. That makes it unusually revealing of **who the candidate
thinks holds the power in a conversation** — which is a culture question, not a growth question,
and it is the part of the read that P&C should own.

Four registers show up. Name which one you are reading before you score anything else, because
it explains most of the rest of the submission.

| Register | How it sounds | What it usually means | Verdict risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Policymaker register** | Frames Taleemabad's work as *the Secretary's* problem being solved. Talks about their targets, their political timeline, their risk of being embarrassed. Asks for a small, safe first step. | The person has actually sat across from government and been told no. Rare and valuable. | This is the bar |
| **Donor register** | Impact language, theory of change, "scalable model", beneficiaries, "we are seeking to partner". Emotive opening about a child. | Their real experience is fundraising or proposal writing, not government relationships. Very common, and the case study warns against it explicitly ("not a donor, not a customer") — so it also reads as instruction-blindness. | Median at best |
| **Vendor register** | Product features, dashboards, app downloads, "our solution", pricing/deployment, a demo. | Their experience is B2B sales into buyers, not partnership with institutions that cannot be sold to. | Below bar for this role |
| **Hero-narrator register** | The candidate is the protagonist of every example. "I turned it around", "I got the Secretary to agree", "I convinced them". The other people in the story have no agency and often no names. | Watch this one carefully. It can co-exist with real competence, and it is a *culture* signal about how they will behave in a room with our team in it. | Flag it in the rating, not just the notes |

**Calibration rule (carried from the Furqan Afzal scorecard, 2026-08-14): hero-narrator /
patronage register belongs in the rating itself, not only in the flags.** If the whole submission
is written as the candidate's personal triumph over other people, that is evidence about how
they will work here, and it should move a score down — not sit politely in a comments box.

---

## What the case study is testing overall

The brief says it mirrors three weekly muscles. Underneath, it is testing four things:

1. **Can they change register for the room?** A1 tests it against a sceptical official, A2
   against a mixed room of peers and seniors, A3 against an institution with a procurement
   cycle. A candidate who writes all three in the same voice has one voice.
2. **Do they know that institutional partnerships are slow, multi-person, and not closed by
   enthusiasm?** A3 asks *where it will stall* — for a reason.
3. **Do they distinguish a moment from a marketing event?** A2 says "not a marketing event"
   out loud. Most submissions will produce a launch event anyway.
4. **Can they be honest about a room that partly did not work?** The 200-word reflection is the
   only place the candidate is not performing for a stakeholder, and it carries the most P&C
   signal per word in the whole pack.

# Assignment 1 — Strategic Storytelling

*Policy one-pager the Secretary reads in three minutes, plus a 5-slide narrative arc.*

## What it was built to expose

The brief hands the candidate a very specific person: **smart, sceptical, short on time, and has
seen a hundred edtech pitches that promised transformation and delivered tablets in cupboards.**
That sentence is the whole test. It is an instruction to write *against* the edtech pitch genre.

The trap is that the genre is the path of least resistance — and if the candidate uses AI for
this, the genre is exactly what comes out. **A1 is the assignment where undisclosed AI is easiest
to spot**, because the default LLM output for "one-pager for a government official" is fluent,
structurally perfect, emotionally generic, and reads like every other one.

The second trap: we gave them only public facts, deliberately thin ones — cartoons on national
TV, a learning app on 1.5 million smartphones, 140 low-fee private schools, two districts with
thousands of teachers and measurable learning gains, Growth recently separated from Fundraising.
**"Measurable learning gains" has no number in it.** What a candidate does with that gap is a
direct integrity read.

## The answer patterns you will see, and what each means

**Pattern A — "The problem is Pakistan's learning crisis."** Opens with out-of-school children,
learning poverty statistics, a national tragedy. Then Taleemabad as the answer.
*What it means:* they are writing to a donor and have not internalised who is reading. A
Secretary of School Education does not need to be told his province has a learning crisis; he is
accountable for it. Opening there reads, to him, as being lectured about his own job. This is the
single most common failure in this assignment and it is not a small one — it is a misread of
power. **Probe it; don't assume incompetence.** Some candidates know better and defaulted under
time pressure.

**Pattern B — "Here is what the app does."** Features, screenshots, download numbers, teacher
dashboard.
*What it means:* vendor register. They are selling a product to a buyer. Almost always a B2B
sales or MNC key-account background — which our validated persona work says is the wrong shape
for this role, so treat it as consistent evidence rather than a one-off slip.

**Pattern C — "The risk of doing this with us is low, and here is the smallest way to start."**
Frames the Secretary's actual exposure: another failed pilot, another donor-funded programme
that ends when the money does, a photo-op that gets him a bad headline. Proposes something small,
reversible, and evidenced — often one district, existing government teachers, government
infrastructure, measured by an independent or pre-agreed instrument.
*What it means:* this person has been in the room. **This is the bar.** They understand that the
official's dominant emotion is not hope, it is risk aversion.

**Pattern D — Fabricates the evidence.** Invents a percentage learning gain, a named study, an
effect size, a "40% improvement in literacy outcomes".
*What it means:* disqualifying-adjacent, and the case study pre-empted it twice ("ground
everything in the facts provided or publicly verifiable information", "if you make an assumption,
label it as one"). A candidate who invents a number for a policymaker briefing will invent one
for a real Secretary. **Distinguish carefully:** a *labelled* placeholder ("assumption: gains in
the range typically reported for structured pedagogy programmes — to be replaced with our actual
study figure") is the opposite signal. That is a strong candidate handling a real gap correctly.
Read the label before you read the number.

**Pattern E — Names the gap and asks for it.** Says plainly: the evidence line is the strongest
asset in this pitch and I was not given the figure, so here is the shape of the claim and the
number I would need from the study team before this goes out.
*What it means:* excellent. Treats a missing input as something to escalate rather than
paper over, which is exactly the behaviour we want on a live brief.

## What to underline

**Strong**
- Written to the reader's risk, not to our mission.
- Evidence handled honestly — figures sourced, gaps labelled, no invented effect sizes.
- The partnership ask is small, concrete and reversible. A first step, not a transformation.
- The 5-slide arc has a *turn* in it — something that changes the Secretary's mind mid-briefing,
  usually the "why the last hundred pitches failed and why this is structurally different" slide.
- Uses the 140 schools and the app history as *proof of scar tissue* (we have run schools, we
  know what breaks) rather than as a résumé.
- Under three minutes of actual reading. Count it — the brief specified it.

**Median**
- Competent, well-organised, correct sections, but interchangeable with any edtech one-pager.
- Slide outline is a table of contents (context → problem → solution → impact → ask) with no
  argument and no turn.
- Public facts restated accurately but not put to work.

**Weak / red flags**
- 🔴 **Invented evidence.** Any unlabelled figure not in the public facts.
- 🔴 **No ask, or an ask that is a meeting.** "We would love to explore how we might work
  together" is not a partnership proposal.
- 🔴 **Sentimentality** — a child's story as the opener, after the brief said "emotionally
  resonant without being sentimental".
- 🟡 One-pager that is three pages. Instruction breach; note it, and note whether they said so.
- 🟡 Slide count wrong (not 5) with no explanation.
- 🟡 **No AI disclosure on prose this fluent.** The brief explicitly permitted AI and asked for
  one line per deliverable. Silence here is a disclosure failure, not an AI failure.

## Probes for the room

1. "Take the one-pager and cut it to a single sentence you'd say to the Secretary in a corridor
   before he gets in the lift. What's the sentence?" — *Strong answers get to the risk-and-proof
   line. Donor-register answers restate the mission.*
2. "He reads it and says: *this is what the last three organisations told me.* What do you say
   next?" — *This is the whole role. Listen for whether they get more evidence, or more
   enthusiastic.*
3. "Where did the evidence number in your one-pager come from?" — *Ask this even if the number is
   labelled. The answer separates careful from lucky, and it is the fastest fabrication check
   you have.*
4. "Which slide is the one where he changes his mind, and what happens if he doesn't?" — *Tests
   whether the arc is an argument or a sequence.*
5. **P&C-owned:** "Who else at Taleemabad would you need in that room, and what would you need
   them to do?" — *A GM who walks into a Secretary's office alone, forever, is a single point of
   failure and usually a hero-narrator. Listen for whether they use colleagues or just cite
   them.*

# Assignment 2 — The Room

*Design a convening around a study showing measurable learning gains. Positioning, not marketing.*

## What it was built to expose

Two things, and candidates almost always get one and miss the other.

**First: does "objective" mean an outcome or an output?** The brief asks *"what must be true the
morning after for this event to have been worth it?"* — phrasing chosen to make an output answer
("150 attendees, media coverage, the study launched") visibly insufficient. The morning-after
frame is asking about *changed positions in named people's heads*, and then about pipeline.

**Second: the follow-up engine is the real assignment.** Anyone who has organised an event can
design an agenda; convening logistics are a commodity skill. The line that actually separates
candidates is *"how conversations become pipeline"* and *"who gets what within 7 days"*. Weak
submissions spend 80% of their space on the agenda and two bullets on follow-up. **Check the
proportions before you read the content — the allocation of effort is itself the signal.**

The stated constraint "**not** a marketing event" is a compliance test in the same way the
Secretary's scepticism was in A1. Most submissions will produce a launch event with a
press-release plan.

## The answer patterns you will see, and what each means

**Pattern A — The launch event.** Chief guest, ribbon, keynote from leadership, media wall,
press release, panel of allies who agree with us, hashtag.
*What it means:* their experience is events/communications, not strategic engagement. They read
"positions Taleemabad as a thought leader" as *publicity* rather than *credibility with peers*.
Not disqualifying — but it is a different job than this one, and the brief told them so.

**Pattern B — The seminar.** Well-structured agenda, sensible sessions, appropriate speakers,
sound room map — and follow-up as "share deck and thank-you notes; schedule follow-up meetings."
*What it means:* competent convener, unproven closer. This is the median submission and it is
genuinely fine. The debrief has to resolve whether the closing muscle exists at all, because the
document won't tell you.

**Pattern C — The working session.** The room is designed so senior people have to *do* something
— react to the findings, respond to a provocation, commit to a position in front of peers. Often
a structured challenge to our own study by someone who might disagree with it. Follow-up is
differentiated by attendee type, with named owners and a 7-day sequence, and the highest-value
conversations are pre-planned before the event rather than harvested after it.
*What it means:* this person has run rooms for a purpose, not for attendance. **This is the bar.**

**Pattern D — The over-engineered room.** 12 attendee types, a matrix, a comms plan, three
workstreams, a risk register. Impressive volume, 2.5 hours of work claimed, and no answer to
what must be true the morning after.
*What it means:* process fluency substituting for judgment — often a consulting or programme-
management background. Probe for prioritisation: a person who cannot tell you which two of the
twelve attendees actually matter has not made a decision.

**Pattern E — Invites the sceptics on purpose.** Puts a known critic, a rival provider, or a
bureaucrat with a bad prior in the room, and says why.
*What it means:* unusually confident and unusually honest. Credibility with peers is built in
front of people who might disagree. Read the *why* — if it is reasoned, this is an excellence
marker; if it is decorative, it is not.

## What to underline

**Strong**
- Morning-after objective stated as a change in named stakeholders' positions, plus a pipeline
  consequence.
- Room map where each attendee type has a *distinct* walk-away, not five variants of "Taleemabad
  is credible."
- An honest answer to the speech-fatigue question — a mechanism (structured reaction, small
  groups, a hard question put to the room, ruthless time-boxing), not "we will keep sessions
  engaging."
- Talking points that are claims leadership can defend, including the limits of the study.
- Follow-up engine with owners, a 7-day sequence, differentiated asks, and how it enters the
  pipeline — ideally connecting to the tracker in A3.
- **Raises attribution or limitation of our own study unprompted** — "measurable learning gains"
  in two districts is not a national claim, and a room of technical people will say so.
- Within limits: 6 slides or 4 pages.

**Median**
- Sound agenda, generic walk-aways, follow-up as a courtesy step.
- Talking points that are mission statements.

**Weak / red flags**
- 🔴 **A marketing event**, after being told not to. Compliance and comprehension both.
- 🔴 **No follow-up engine**, or one with no owners and no dates — the section the brief weighted
  most heavily.
- 🔴 **Over-claiming the study.** Presenting two-district gains as proof of a national model, or
  inventing figures for the findings.
- 🟡 Room is all allies. Nobody in it could say anything uncomfortable.
- 🟡 Over limits (>6 slides / >4 pages), unflagged.
- 🟡 Attendee list is job titles with no reason attached.

## Probes for the room

1. "It's the morning after. What one email is in your inbox that tells you it worked?" —
   *Concrete answers name a person and a request. Weak answers describe coverage.*
2. "Your budget is cut in half and the room is 15 people, not 100. Who stays?" — *Forces the
   prioritisation the document usually avoids. Strong candidates already know their two people.*
3. "A senior official in the room says the sample is too small to mean anything. It's a fair
   point. What happens next?" — *Tests intellectual honesty under status pressure, live. Watch
   whether they defend, deflect, or concede-and-reframe.*
4. "Walk me through day 3 after the event. Who is doing what?" — *Follow-up engines that were
   written to fill a section collapse here.*
5. **P&C-owned:** "Who in the organisation do you need to make this happen, and what are you
   asking of them?" — *A convening is cross-functional. Listen for whether other teams are
   collaborators or resources.*

# Assignment 3 — The Pipeline

*Five real prospective partners, a tracker, and one deal mapped end to end.*

## What it was built to expose

This is the only assignment with a verifiable component, and therefore the fastest lie detector
in the pack. **Verify the five organisations exist, that their remit is roughly as described, and
that their current status matches what the candidate claims.** Naming a donor or programme whose
Pakistan portfolio has materially changed — or a body that has been restructured or wound down —
without noting it is a desk-research currency signal: the sector moves, and a Growth Manager who
does not track it will walk into a meeting with a stale map. Do not assert this from memory in
the report; check it, then say what you checked.

The intended read of Part 3 is one sentence in the brief: **"where it will probably stall."** A
candidate who cannot answer that has not closed an institutional deal. Optimistic pipelines are
written by people who have only ever been in the early, pleasant part of the cycle.

Part 2's tracker is a small honesty test of its own: *what "progress" means for each in the next
30 days.* Institutional partnerships mostly do not move in 30 days, so the honest answer is often
"progress is a named person confirmed and a document requested", not "MoU signed." A tracker
promising five signed agreements in 30 days is a person who has never watched a government
approval move.

## The answer patterns you will see, and what each means

**Pattern A — The donor list.** Five funders: the multilaterals, the bilaterals, the big
foundations. Opening conversation is a concept note.
*What it means:* they are still fundraising. The case study told them Growth and Fundraising were
recently separated, and the brief asked for institutional *partners* across government *and* the
development sector. An all-donor list means they either did not read that or cannot tell the
difference — and that distinction is the reason this role exists.

**Pattern B — Government-only, no entry point.** Names the right departments and authorities, but
the opening conversation is "request a meeting with the Secretary."
*What it means:* correct map, no route. They know who matters and not how anything actually
begins. Very common in candidates who have observed government from a distance.

**Pattern C — Mixed, with entry points and a reason.** Government bodies alongside development
and sector partners, each with a specific hook — an existing programme our work plugs into, a
target they are behind on, a technical need we already serve. Opening conversations are specific
and small.
*What it means:* this is the bar. **Look especially for whether they identify an intermediary or
warm route rather than a cold approach** — institutional partnerships are almost always
introduced, and knowing that is experience talking.

**Pattern D — Stalls named precisely.** Says the deal will die in procurement, or at a transfer
of the sponsoring official, or at the budget cycle boundary, or in the gap between a supportive
Secretary and an unconvinced technical team — and has a specific response to each, usually
multi-threading beyond the single champion.
*What it means:* strong. **Officer transfer is the single most telling item on this list.**
Government counterparts move, and a candidate who has lost a deal that way builds for it without
being asked. If they name it unprompted, weight it heavily.

**Pattern E — The optimistic close.** First conversation → proposal → pilot → MoU → scale, with
timelines in weeks, no decision-makers beyond the champion, and "maintain regular follow-up" as
the momentum strategy.
*What it means:* no institutional closing experience, whatever the CV says. Probe hard before
concluding, but this pattern rarely survives a probe.

**Pattern F — Names our own constraint.** Says something like: our two-district evidence base
limits us to partners who will accept a pilot, so three of these five are 12-month conversations,
not this quarter's.
*What it means:* commercial honesty about our own position, unprompted. Excellence marker.

## What to underline

**Strong**
- Five real, verifiable, *relevant* organisations — mixed government and development, not five of
  the same kind.
- Entry points that are specific and human — a person, a programme, an existing forum, a warm
  introduction — not "send an introductory email."
- A tracker with owners, stages, next actions, and a **credible** 30-day definition of progress.
- Stall points named concretely (procurement, officer transfer, budget cycle, technical
  gatekeepers, political timeline) with a response to each.
- Multi-threading beyond the single champion, ideally *with the champion's knowledge*.
- Momentum strategy that gives the counterpart something rather than asking for something —
  the follow-up that carries value, not the follow-up that checks in.
- Assumptions labelled, as instructed.
- Within limits: spreadsheet + max 2-page note.

**Median**
- Real organisations, thin fit rationale, generic openings.
- Tracker present and usable but with optimistic 30-day targets.
- Stall risk acknowledged in general terms ("bureaucratic delays").

**Weak / red flags**
- 🔴 **Organisations that do not exist, or whose remit is fabricated.** Verify all five. This is
  the one part of the case where fabrication is provable, and the brief said "use real
  organizations and public information."
- 🔴 **No stall analysis**, when the brief asked for it in those words.
- 🔴 **Five donors** presented as institutional partnerships.
- 🟡 Tracker is a formatted table with nothing meaningful in it.
- 🟡 Deal path with one decision-maker in it.
- 🟡 30-day targets that no government process could meet, stated with confidence.
- 🟡 Stale sector knowledge — a named body or programme whose status has materially changed,
  presented as current.

## Probes for the room

1. "Of your five, which one is genuinely warm — where you or someone you know could get a
   meeting this month?" — *The honest answer is sometimes "none of them", and that is a fine
   answer, given honestly. Listen for whether the network is real or researched.*
2. "You said it stalls at [X]. Tell me about a time it actually stalled on you, and what
   happened." — *The best question in this whole guide. A real story lands immediately; a
   theoretical one dissolves.*
3. "Your sponsor is transferred out three months in. Monday morning — what do you do?" — *Ask
   this even if they named transfer risk. Tests whether the answer is a plan or a phrase.*
4. "What's the smallest thing you could ask this partner for that they'd say yes to in two
   weeks?" — *Closers think in small reversible steps; pitchers think in MoUs.*
5. "Where did you get your information on [organisation]? When did you last check?" — *Currency
   check, gently. Also a fabrication check.*
6. **P&C-owned:** "Which of these five would you hand to someone else on the team, and why?" —
   *Tests whether they can share a pipeline. Growth Managers who hold every relationship
   personally create risk we have to carry later.*

# The Reflective Response

*200 words or fewer: a room you led. What made it work, and what would you do differently.*

## What it was built to expose

**This is the highest-signal-per-word item in the pack and the part P&C should read first.** It is
the only place the candidate is not writing to a stakeholder, and the only place we asked for a
verifiable claim about their own past. The question is deliberately two-sided — *what made it
work* invites the highlight reel, *what would you do differently* asks for the cost.

A 200-word cap and a voice-note option were both offered, which means format tells you something
too: someone who sends 600 words has told you how they treat a stated constraint, and someone who
sends a voice note has told you they are comfortable being unpolished.

## The answer patterns you will see, and what each means

**Pattern A — The triumph.** A hard room, senior people, and the candidate turned it around
single-handed. The "differently" line is a humility ornament: "I would have prepared even more
thoroughly."
*What it means:* hero-narrator. Nothing was learned because nothing was admitted. Per the
calibration rule, this belongs in the rating, not just the notes — it is a prediction about how
they will describe our team's work later.

**Pattern B — The specific, costly admission.** A concrete room, a real mistake with a real
consequence — the wrong people invited, the senior person who dominated and shut the room down,
the follow-up that never happened, the objection they did not anticipate. Names what it cost and
what they changed afterwards.
*What it means:* this is the bar. **Weight the *consequence*, not the *confession*.** Anyone can
name a flaw; a strong answer names what it cost someone.

**Pattern C — Generic wisdom.** "The key to a successful convening is knowing your audience and
preparing thoroughly." No room, no date, no people.
*What it means:* either no such room exists, or AI wrote it. Either way, unusable as evidence —
and the whole question was a request for evidence. Ask for the room directly in the debrief; if
it does not materialise in specifics, you have your answer.

**Pattern D — Other people have agency in it.** Colleagues are named, a counterpart is quoted, a
junior person's contribution matters, the candidate is not the only competent person present.
*What it means:* the strongest culture signal available in this pack. It costs nothing to write
and almost nobody does it.

**Pattern E — Over the limit.** 400+ words, or a 5-minute voice note.
*What it means:* a note, not a verdict — but pair it with over-length elsewhere. A pattern of
ignoring stated constraints across four deliverables is a pattern.

## Probes for the room

1. "Tell me more about that room. Who else was in it?" — *Specifics arrive instantly for real
   memories. Names, roles, what was at stake.*
2. "You said you'd do [X] differently. Who paid for that not going well the first time?" —
   *Separates a real cost from a decorative one. This is the P&C question in this section.*
3. "When did you realise it was going wrong — during, or afterwards?" — *In-room detection is a
   much higher bar than hindsight, and it is what this role needs.*
4. "Who taught you how to run a room like that?" — *Warm, disarming, and it tells you whether
   they learn from people or believe they arrived fully formed.*

# Scoring dimensions (GM variant)

The SMG rubric does not transfer — this case has no dataset, so *data judgment* is replaced.
Six dimensions, each 1–5, weighted to 100. **Every score cites a quoted line, slide or page.**

| # | Dimension | Weight | What it reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Register & audience judgment** | 25% | A1 one-pager + A2 talking points — did they write to the reader's world or ours |
| 2 | **Narrative architecture** | 15% | A1 slide arc — is there an argument with a turn, or a table of contents |
| 3 | **Convening design for outcome** | 20% | A2 objective, room map, and especially the follow-up engine |
| 4 | **Institutional deal craft** | 25% | A3 — real partners, real entry points, named stall points, multi-threading |
| 5 | **Integrity & instruction discipline** | 10% | Evidence honesty, labelled assumptions, limits respected, AI disclosed |
| 6 | **Self-awareness** | 5% | The reflection — specificity, cost, agency of others |

## Flags — these override the total

| Flag | Severity | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Fabricated evidence or organisations** | Disqualifying | Invented figures for the study, or partners that do not exist / whose remit is invented |
| **Undisclosed AI** | Serious | Fluent generic prose, no disclosure line, brief explicitly asked for one |
| **Materially incomplete** | Serious | A required deliverable missing (both A1 parts, A2 brief, A3 spreadsheet, reflection) |
| **Hero-narrator register** | Serious | Sustained across submission; move the rating, per the 2026-08-14 calibration rule |
| **Wrong register throughout** | Serious | Donor or vendor voice in all four deliverables — one voice, and it is not this role's |
| **Instruction breach** | Note | Over page/slide/word limits, wrong format, unlabelled assumptions |
| **Stale sector knowledge** | Note | Named body/programme whose status has materially changed, presented as current |

## Bands

| Total | Band | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 80+ | Strong yes | Proceed to debrief; probe the excellence markers they missed |
| 65–79 | Yes | Proceed; debrief targets the weakest dimension |
| 50–64 | Borderline | Proceed only if the pool is thin; state what the debrief must resolve |
| <50 | No | Do not proceed; give the specific reason |

**Never force a fixed shortlist size.** If three clear the bar, recommend three and say so.

# The traps — what this case is really testing

| Trap | Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|---|
| **The Secretary wants to be inspired** | Learning-crisis statistics, a child's story, transformation language | Writes to his risk: another failed pilot with his name on it |
| **Public facts are a résumé** | Lists cartoons, app, 140 schools, two districts | Uses the 140 schools as evidence we know what breaks in a classroom |
| **"Measurable learning gains" needs a number** | Invents one | Labels the gap and says which number they would need before sending |
| **Thought leadership = publicity** | Launch event, media wall, press release, hashtag | A working room of peers where positions actually change |
| **Agenda is the assignment** | 80% agenda, two bullets of follow-up | Follow-up engine with owners and a 7-day sequence, tied to the pipeline |

| **Institutional partners = donors** | Five funders and a concept note | Government and sector mixed, each with a specific hook |

# Excellence markers — above the bar, not merely at it

1. **Names the evidence gap in A1 and asks for the figure** rather than filling it.
2. **Writes A1 to the Secretary's political timeline** — what he can show, and when.
3. **The 5-slide arc has a genuine turn** — a slide whose job is to change a mind.
4. **Raises the limits of our own study before the room does** — two districts is not a national
   claim.
5. **Designs A2 so senior people commit to a position in front of peers**, and says why that
   converts better than a keynote.
6. **Invites someone who might disagree**, with a reason.
7. **The follow-up engine differentiates what each layer gets from what each layer gives.**
8. **Identifies a warm route into at least one of the five partners**, not a cold approach.
9. **Names officer transfer as the primary risk** to a long institutional cycle, unprompted.
10. **Multi-threads with the champion's knowledge**, not behind their back.
11. **Says something commercially uncomfortable about us** — e.g. three of these five are
    12-month conversations given our current evidence base.
12. **Other people have agency in the reflection.**

# What People & Culture specifically owns in this debrief

The growth read and the culture read pull from the same document but ask different questions. If
you are carrying 50% of the decision, these five are yours — and none of them are answered by the
submission alone, which is exactly why the debrief exists.

1. **Register and power.** Who does this person think holds power in a room, and how do they
   behave towards someone who has it? A1 and A2 answer this in writing; the probes confirm it
   live.
2. **Agency of others.** Do colleagues, counterparts and juniors exist as people in their
   examples, or as instruments? Read the reflection first, then A2's cross-functional asks.
3. **Honesty under status pressure.** The "your sample is too small" probe is a live test. Watch
   whether they defend, deflect, or concede and reframe.
4. **Constraint response.** Four deliverables had explicit limits (3-minute read, 5 slides, 6
   slides/4 pages, 2 pages, 200 words, AI disclosure). How someone treats a stated constraint
   when nobody is watching predicts a great deal.
5. **Shareability of relationships.** A Growth Manager who holds every institutional relationship
   personally is a risk the organisation absorbs later. The "which of these five would you hand
   over" probe is the cleanest read available.
