# Case Study: Securing the Extension — or the Renewal

**Role family:** Growth / Government Partnerships
**Created:** 2026-07-31 (curated by Coco from an internal conversation on the federal training-institute program's extension/renewal journey; Ayesha approved the framing)
**Confidentiality:** Institute is anonymized and all real budget figures are excluded, as agreed in the source conversation. Do NOT add real numbers or expose internal workarounds when sharing with candidates.

## The Scenario (candidate-facing)

Taleemabad runs a coaching program inside a federal teacher-training institute in Islamabad, funded through a government-approved project. The project term is now approaching its end date, and the program's continuity — including the coaches and support staff currently working on contract — depends entirely on what happens next.

**Your central task: How will you get this project extended, or renewed? You own both the choice of path and its execution, end to end.**

There are two possible paths:

**Path 1 — Extension.** Stay within the existing project's scope and budget. This means working the numbers first: what funds remain in the project envelope, what the operational cost, HR cost, and OPEX look like for the extension period, and whether the additional work can genuinely be managed within the money already allocated. If it can, you must build the case and take it — first to the host institute (the primary stakeholder), and then, with the host institute alongside you, to the Ministry. The Ministry's default position will be: *no additional budget — fit it inside what you already have.*

**Path 2 — Renewal.** Secure the program's next chapter outright: either a fresh renewal of the project for 3–5 years (with funds allocated in one go and released year by year), or regularization — a specific annual budget permanently assigned to the host institute to cover coach salaries every year. This path runs through a PC-1 that must clear three layers: the institute's Finance Department, the Ministry of Education (Secretary-level approval), and finally the Planning Commission, where annual budgets are scrutinized, questioned, and cut.

One more reality: at none of these offices does anyone consider your file *their* job. The FD won't chase it, the Ministry won't draft for you, and the Planning Commission won't move unless someone makes it move. You are one person, and this work needs ten hands.

## The Questions

1. **How will you get the project extended?** Walk us through your approach from day one: where you start, how you build the case, and how you carry it through each office until the extension is secured.
2. **Extension or renewal — which path do you pursue, and why?** Or a sequenced combination — walk us through how you'd decide, and what information you'd need in hand before deciding.
3. **How do you move the file?** Map your stakeholder journey step by step: who you approach first, what you present at each stage, what you do yourself versus what you wait for others to do, and how you get into rooms when the person you need "has no time." Be specific about how you'd build the relationships that make doors open.
4. **Build the money case.** The contracted team's costs include salaries, operational, and logistics costs. Prepare the costing for the next 2–3 years and the pitch that goes with it: What changes year over year, and why? What figure do you ask for, knowing the FD negotiates first, the Ministry negotiates second, and the Planning Commission negotiates third — and how do you justify that figure at each table?

## Evaluator's Guide (internal — NOT shared with candidate)

A strong answer shows, in this order of importance:

1. **Full-chain ownership.** They plan to do the FD's legwork, the Ministry's drafting, and the Planning Commission's follow-up themselves — finding the right POC at each layer. Anyone who says "the Ministry will take it forward from there" or "writing the PC-1 is their department's job" has failed the core test.
2. **Costing realism.** The next-3-years budget must NOT be flat. If they carry last year's budget forward unchanged — no inflation, no appraisals, no rising operational charges — they won't manage this role. The costing should also be justifiable line by line, not padded arbitrarily.
3. **Negotiation buffer.** They should pitch above their true requirement, knowing three successive rounds of cuts stand between the ask and the final allocation — and they should be able to defend where the buffer sits.
4. **Relationship craft.** Look for concrete, human tactics for access and goodwill (the PA/PS who tips you off that "madam has arrived, come in ten minutes"), not abstract talk of "stakeholder engagement."
5. **Rules literacy.** Awareness that additional budget allocations are governed by clauses and rules they must learn proactively — asking around, reading, noting — rather than discovering them mid-process.
