# Fundraising Capability and Workshop Guide

**Taleemabad | People and Culture**
**Version 1.0 | 6 September 2026**

---

## How to use this guide

This is a working document for a practical capability-building workshop, not a training manual to be read cover to cover. It is written so that the person leading the session can open it on the day and run from it.

It does three things:

1. Sets out what a strong institutional fundraising team actually needs to be able to do, in twelve capabilities.
2. Gives a ready-to-run workshop plan of roughly two and a half hours, with objectives, talking points, exercises and expected outputs.
3. Turns the workshop into work, through a capability assessment matrix and a 30 to 60 day application plan.

**A note on naming.** This guide uses roles rather than individual names, so it can be reused with any future team member. Read them as follows:

| Label used here | Who it means |
|---|---|
| **Workshop lead** | The Senior Manager, Fundraising, who runs the session |
| **Track A** | The team member whose primary development is donor intelligence and relationships |
| **Track B** | The team member whose primary development is opportunity and proposal development |

**A note on numbers.** Every figure in the worked exercise is left blank on purpose. Before the workshop, the workshop lead should fill them in with real numbers confirmed by Finance and Programmes. Using invented numbers in a capability exercise teaches the team to be comfortable with invented numbers, which is exactly the habit we are trying to prevent.

# Part 1. What fundraising actually is

The most common mistake in a young fundraising team is to treat fundraising as proposal writing. Proposal writing is one stage out of eleven, and it is the stage where the outcome has usually already been decided by how well the earlier stages were done.

The full journey looks like this:

> **Research → Qualify → Cultivate → Design the Ask → Proposal → Budget → Due Diligence → Close → Deliver and Report → Steward → Renew**

This should become the team's shared language. When someone says "we are at cultivation with this funder," everyone should know what that means, what happens next, and who owns it.

For every stage, the workshop lead should be able to answer four questions:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What is the objective of this stage? | Stops activity that has no purpose |
| What output does the team produce? | Makes the stage checkable |
| Who owns it? | Prevents work falling between people |
| What does good quality look like? | Makes feedback specific instead of personal |

### Where teams typically lose money

| Stage | Common failure |
|---|---|
| Research | Finding many funders, understanding none of them |
| Qualify | Pursuing an attractive funder who was never a realistic fit |
| Cultivate | Pitching before understanding what the funder cares about |
| Proposal | Writing what we want to say instead of what they asked for |
| Budget | A number the fundraiser cannot defend |
| Due diligence | Being asked for evidence or policies we have not prepared |
| Report | Treating the grant as finished once the money arrives |
| Renew | Going quiet, then reappearing when we need money again |

# Part 2. The eleven questions

By the end of this workshop, both team members should be able to answer these eleven questions about any Taleemabad programme, without reading from a deck.

1. Who should fund this?
2. Why are we a fit for them?
3. What exactly are we asking them to fund?
4. What problem are we solving?
5. Does our solution work?
6. How do we know?
7. What does it cost?
8. Can it scale?
9. What would additional funding enable?
10. Why Taleemabad and not someone else?
11. How will we build and maintain this relationship?

> **Use this as the opening exercise.** Ask the questions cold. Anything the team cannot answer goes on a flipchart headed "Things the fundraising team must learn." That list becomes the real development plan, and it is more honest than any self-assessment.

# Part 3. The twelve core capabilities

Each capability below has three parts: what it means, what good looks like, and how we build it.

## 1. Organisational knowledge

**What it means.** Knowing Taleemabad well enough to explain it to a stranger without notes: mission, history, programmes, geography, who we serve, our partners, our current funders, our outcomes, our strategic priorities, what we are scaling now, and what makes us different from other education organisations.

**What good looks like.** Can explain Taleemabad in sixty seconds, then answer the follow-up question. Knows which claims we can defend and which we cannot.

**How we build it.** Two exercises. First, the sixty second explanation. Second, and separately, why someone should fund us. These are different questions, and confusing them is the most common weakness in new fundraisers.

## 2. Donor research

**What it means.** Finding and understanding potential funders: foundations, institutional donors, corporate social responsibility partners, individual philanthropists and pooled funds.

**What good looks like.** For any named funder, can answer: what do they fund, where do they fund, what size of grant do they give, what have they funded before, what does their published strategy tell us, how do organisations actually access them, who are the relevant people, and do we have any warm connection.

**How we build it.** Give a real funder and twenty minutes. Reward the best judgement, not the most pages of notes.

## 3. Donor qualification

**What it means.** Deciding whether a funder is worth pursuing before we spend three weeks on them.

**What good looks like.** Can say no to an attractive funder with a clear reason, and can defend a yes.

**How we build it.** Use the Donor Fit Score below. Score each dimension from 1 to 5.

| Dimension | The question to answer |
|---|---|
| Mission fit | Does the funder fund the problem we solve? |
| Geography fit | Do they fund Pakistan, and our specific regions? |
| Programme fit | Do our programmes match their stated priorities? |
| Evidence fit | Do we have the kind of evidence they expect? |
| Scale fit | Does our scale ambition match theirs? |
| Ticket size fit | Is the likely grant meaningful for us? |
| Relationship strength | Do we have access, or a warm route in? |
| Timing | Is there a live opportunity now? |
| Effort | Is the opportunity worth the work required? |

The workshop lead then makes one of four calls:

> **Pursue** (act now) · **Cultivate** (build the relationship, no ask yet) · **Monitor** (watch for a change) · **Decline** (say no and record why)

Recording why we declined matters. In a year, someone will ask about that funder again.

## 4. Stakeholder mapping

**What it means.** Understanding the people around a funding decision, not just the institution.

**What good looks like.** For each priority funder, can name the decision maker, the influencer, the champion, the gatekeeper and the connector, and can say what each of them cares about, who knows whom, who should approach whom, and what the next move is.

**How we build it.** Map one live funder on a whiteboard together. The output is not a diagram, it is a next action with a name against it.

## 5. Donor engagement and listening

**What it means.** Cultivation: building understanding and trust before making an ask.

**What good looks like.** Enters a first conversation to learn, not to pitch. Comes out able to say what the funder actually cares about, in the funder's own words.

**How we build it.** Teach four questions and then practise them:

- What priorities are most important to your foundation right now?
- What kinds of education investments are you most interested in?
- What evidence matters when you assess an organisation?
- What usually stops you from funding an organisation?

Then listen. The role-play rule below is the fastest way to teach this: no pitching for the first five minutes.

## 6. Storytelling and pitching

**What it means.** Building a case for support that combines a human story, evidence and economics.

**What good looks like.** Neither "this child's life changed" on its own, nor "learning outcomes rose by eighteen per cent" on its own. Both, connected.

**How we build it.** Use this structure:

| Element | The question |
|---|---|
| The problem | What is happening? |
| The people | Who experiences it? |
| The stakes | Why does it matter? |
| The intervention | What are we doing differently? |
| The evidence | What shows it works? |
| The ask | What would this funder's money enable? |
| The horizon | What happens at scale? |

## 7. Proposal and grant development

**What it means.** Turning a qualified opportunity into a compliant, evidence-backed, donor-specific proposal.

**What good looks like.** Never starts with a blank document. Starts with the funder's own requirements.

**How we build it.** Give a real request for proposals and run four steps:

1. Highlight every requirement in the document.
2. Build a compliance matrix: requirement, where we answer it, who owns it, status.
3. Write a one page proposal skeleton.
4. List what information is missing.

Step four is the one that separates a strong fundraiser from a weak one. A strong fundraiser says "I need Finance to confirm this" or "I need Programmes to give me this figure." A weak one invents an answer.

The content areas to cover over time: eligibility, concept notes, problem statements, theory of change, objectives, activities, outputs, outcomes, indicators, implementation plan, evidence, risk, sustainability, scale, and the funding ask itself.

## 8. Financial literacy for fundraisers

**What it means.** Understanding the numbers well enough to present and defend them. This is expanded in Part 5.

**What good looks like.** Can answer "why does this cost what it costs" without calling Finance mid-meeting.

**How we build it.** A dedicated session with Finance in the room, using one real programme budget.

## 9. Evidence and monitoring literacy

**What it means.** Understanding the evidence we are selling, including its limits.

**What good looks like.** Knows the difference between output, outcome and impact. Knows what our data can support and what it cannot. Says "we do not have that evidence yet" when that is the truth.

**How we build it.** Take one real result we quote to funders and trace it back: where did this number come from, what was measured, over what period, compared to what.

Vocabulary the team needs: output, outcome, impact, indicator, baseline, endline, theory of change, comparison group, attribution, cost per person reached, cost per outcome, evidence quality.

They do not need to become researchers. They do need to stop repeating numbers they cannot source.

## 10. Cost-effectiveness and scale

**What it means.** Understanding impact relative to cost, and the pathway from a working programme to a programme at national scale.

**What good looks like.** When asked "is two thousand rupees per child good," the correct first answer is "we do not know yet," followed by the four questions that would settle it: what outcome is being achieved, what alternatives exist, what do those alternatives cost, and what additional improvement did we generate compared to doing nothing.

**How we build it.** See the funder criteria in Part 6. Several of our priority funders assess exactly this.

## 11. Pipeline management

**What it means.** Fundraising as an operation, not a series of heroic efforts.

**What good looks like.** At any moment, anyone can see what is live, what it is worth, whose it is, and what happens next.

**How we build it.** Maintain one pipeline with these fields as a minimum: donor, opportunity, owner, estimated value, stage, probability, deadline, latest interaction, next action, next action date, proposal status, decision, reporting deadline.

The workshop lead owns the quality of this pipeline. A pipeline that is not current is worse than no pipeline, because it creates false confidence.

## 12. Stewardship and ethics

**What it means.** Everything that happens after the money arrives, and the standards that govern all of it.

**What good looks like.** The cycle continues: acknowledge, deliver, communicate, report, demonstrate impact, maintain the relationship, open the renewal conversation. The funder hears from us when we are not asking for anything.

**The three rules that are not negotiable:**

> Never exaggerate impact to win funding.
> Never use an impressive statistic we cannot source and defend.
> Never promise a use of funds that Programmes or Finance cannot actually deliver.

Honouring donor intent and reporting costs and results accurately are professional obligations, not preferences. A fundraiser who overstates once has damaged every future ask the organisation makes.

# Part 4. Primary development tracks

These are **primary areas of ownership, not exclusive ones.** Both team members are expected to develop working knowledge across the whole cycle. The split exists so that depth builds somewhere, not so that anyone is excused from a stage.

## Track A: Donor intelligence and relationships

Deeper development in capabilities 2 to 5.

- Donor and prospect research
- Prospect qualification and the Donor Fit Score
- Stakeholder mapping around priority funders
- Cultivation, discovery conversations and outreach
- Relationship management and follow-up discipline

**What we should see in ninety days.** A qualified prospect list with scored fit, a stakeholder map for the top three funders, and at least two discovery conversations held and written up.

## Track B: Opportunity and proposal development

Deeper development in capabilities 6 to 9.

- Opportunity and request for proposal analysis
- Concept notes and problem statements
- Proposal writing and coordination across Programmes and Finance
- Evidence integration
- Submission management and compliance

**What we should see in ninety days.** One compliance matrix built from a real request for proposals, one concept note drafted end to end, and one submission managed to deadline without a last-minute scramble.

## Shared by both

Financial literacy, evidence literacy, storytelling, pipeline discipline, stewardship and ethics. Neither track is exempt from any of these.

# Part 5. What fundraisers need to know financially

Proposal budgeting is one of the hardest parts of grant development, and it is where credibility is most often lost. Fundraisers do not need to replace Finance. They need to understand and defend the numbers they put in front of a donor.

## The concepts

| Concept | Why a fundraiser needs it |
|---|---|
| Programme cost | The total picture behind any ask |
| Direct cost | What the donor can see themselves funding |
| Indirect cost | The overhead conversation, which we must be able to justify |
| Fixed and variable cost | Explains what happens to cost when volume changes |
| Cost per child, school, teacher | The unit a donor thinks in |
| Cost per outcome | The unit a serious donor thinks in |
| Cost drivers | What actually moves the total |
| Budget assumptions | The reason a number is what it is |
| Restricted and unrestricted funding | Determines what the money can be used for |
| Co-funding | Who else is in, and what that signals |
| Funding gap | The honest size of the need |
| In-kind contribution | Value that is not cash |
| Cost recovery | Whether we are covering our own costs |
| Budget variance | The gap between plan and actual, which reporting will expose |

## The questions a donor will ask

The team should be able to answer each of these, or say precisely who they need to confirm it with.

- Why does this programme cost this amount?
- What is our cost per child?
- What would another ten million rupees, or another hundred thousand dollars, enable?
- What happens if the donor funds only half of the request?
- How will costs change at scale? Do they fall, and why?
- Which of these costs are direct and which are indirect?
- What does Finance need to validate before we submit?

> **Workshop rule.** "I will confirm that with Finance and come back to you today" is a strong answer. A guess is not.

# Part 6. What our priority funders actually look for

This section exists because generic fundraising training does not prepare anyone for these four conversations. Each of these funders publishes what it assesses. Read the published criteria before the workshop and use them as the standard.

| Funder | What they assess | What that means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Mulago Foundation** | Whether a model is good enough, big enough, simple enough and cheap enough to scale. Good enough includes consequential impact and high quality evidence. Cheap enough asks whether the eventual payer can and will actually pay for it. | We must be able to name the doer at scale and the payer at scale, not just describe our programme. |
| **GiveWell** | Evidence of effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, room for more funding, and transparency. | We must be able to say what additional money specifically enables, not just that we need it. |
| **DIV Fund (Development Innovation Ventures)** | Evidence of impact, cost-effectiveness and a credible pathway to durable scale. Considers unit cost, per-person impact, comparison with alternative approaches, theory of change, and whether the intervention can realistically reach scale. Later-stage funding expects evidence that costs and delivery systems hold up at scale. | Our economics, and our comparison against alternatives, must be prepared in advance rather than assembled during an application. |
| **Vitol Foundation** | Funds Early Childhood Care and Education, Adolescent Health, Humanitarian Emergencies, and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene. Looks for work that strengthens performance, access and inclusion, innovation and access to financing, delivered within local systems rather than around them. Does not accept unsolicited applications: organisations are invited to apply by a programme manager. Offers restricted funding within project budgets or fully unrestricted support, and describes itself as a flexible funder that can bridge gaps as organisations move toward scale. The process typically takes three to six months. | Route in and relationship matter more than a strong written application, because there is no open application round. This is a cultivation problem, not a proposal problem. |

## Two concepts worth teaching explicitly

**Room for more funding.** A funder does not only ask whether we are good. They ask what additional money will actually enable. GiveWell has paused or declined funding for organisations it rated well, on the judgement that they could not productively use more money at that moment. So we should never simply say "we need five hundred thousand dollars." We should be able to say what the next five hundred thousand dollars adds: how many additional schools, teachers and children, and what outcomes we expect from them.

**Who delivers and who pays at scale.** If our intervention works, someone eventually has to run it nationally and someone has to pay for it: government, philanthropy, customers or a major aid actor. Both Mulago and DIV push hard on this. These two questions look simple and are not, and a fundraiser who has not thought about them will struggle in a serious donor meeting.

# Part 7. The workshop plan

**Total time: approximately 2 hours 30 minutes.** Heavily practical. The lead should be talking for less than a third of it.

**Materials to prepare in advance:**

- One real request for proposals
- One real programme budget, cleared with Finance
- One funder profile per participant, drawn from Mulago, GiveWell, DIV or Vitol
- A flipchart headed "Things the fundraising team must learn"
- Printed copies of the Donor Fit Score and the capability matrix

---

## Section 1. How fundraising actually works | 15 minutes

**Objective.** Replace "fundraising equals proposal writing" with the full journey.

**Talking points.**

- Walk the eleven stages: Research, Qualify, Cultivate, Design the Ask, Proposal, Budget, Due Diligence, Close, Deliver and Report, Steward, Renew.
- Most proposals are won or lost before the writing starts.
- Introduce the four questions per stage: objective, output, owner, quality bar.

**Exercise.** Each person marks on the journey where they currently spend most of their time, and where they think we are weakest as a team.

**Expected output.** A shared view of the two or three weakest stages, written up. This shapes everything that follows.

---

## Section 2. Knowing Taleemabad | 20 minutes

**Objective.** Expose gaps in organisational knowledge before any donor work begins.

**Talking points.** Rapid-fire, no notes allowed. What problem do we solve? Who do we serve? What is our core intervention? How many people do we reach? What is our evidence? Why does it work? What does it cost? What is scalable about it? Who funds us today? Why should someone fund us?

**Exercise.** A sixty second explanation of Taleemabad from each person, then a separate sixty seconds on why someone should fund us. Treat them as two distinct answers.

**Expected output.** A completed "Things the fundraising team must learn" list, with an owner and a date against each item.

---

## Section 3. Donor research and qualification | 25 minutes

**Objective.** Move from collecting information to making a decision.

**Talking points.** What we need to know about any funder. Introduce the Donor Fit Score and the four calls: Pursue, Cultivate, Monitor, Decline.

**Exercise.** Give each person one funder from Mulago, GiveWell, DIV or Vitol, and twenty minutes. They must answer one question: should Taleemabad pursue this funder, and why or why not.

**Expected output.** One completed Donor Fit Score per person and a recommendation with a reason. Reward the best judgement, not the most information found.

> **For the lead.** If someone recommends pursuing Vitol by submitting a written application, that is the teaching moment: Vitol does not accept unsolicited applications. The right answer is a route in, not a proposal.

---

## Section 4. Building the funding case | 30 minutes

**Objective.** Assemble research, evidence, economics and story into one coherent case. This is the heart of the workshop.

**Talking points.** The seven part story structure from capability 6. The difference between a story with no evidence and evidence with no story.

**Exercise.** Take one real Taleemabad intervention. In pairs or individually, answer the nine questions in Part 8.

**Expected output.** A one page funding case, plus an explicit list of what information is missing and who owns getting it.

---

## Section 5. Finance and evidence | 25 minutes

**Objective.** Build enough financial and evidence literacy to survive donor questioning.

**Talking points.** Direct and indirect costs, unit costs, restricted and unrestricted funding, and what cost-effectiveness actually means. Output, outcome and impact.

**Exercise.** Using the prepared programme figures: calculate cost per child; state what further information is needed to calculate cost per outcome; answer "what would another twenty million rupees enable"; and list which numbers Finance must confirm before anything goes to a donor.

**Expected output.** One calculated unit cost, one list of Finance confirmations required, and one honest statement of what our evidence does not yet support.

---

## Section 6. Donor role-play | 25 minutes

**Objective.** Practise listening first, then defending the case under pressure.

**Setup.** The workshop lead plays the donor. **Rule: no pitching for the first five minutes.** The fundraiser's only job in those five minutes is to understand the donor.

**Talking points.** After the first five minutes, debrief with one question: based on what you just learned, what would you pitch differently? Then run the pressure questions from Part 9.

**Expected output.** Each person names one thing they would change in their pitch based on what they heard, not on what they had prepared.

---

## Section 7. Ownership and development commitments | 10 minutes

**Objective.** Convert the session into assigned work.

**Talking points.** Confirm the two tracks. Confirm that tracks are primary, not exclusive.

**Exercise.** Each person completes their capability matrix: current score, target score, and one development action per capability they are prioritising.

**Expected output.** Completed capability matrices and the 30 to 60 day plan in Part 11, agreed with dates.

---

# Part 8. Worked exercise: build the case

Use one real Taleemabad intervention. Answer these nine questions in order.

1. Who should fund this, and why them specifically?
2. Why are we a fit for that funder, in their own stated terms?
3. What exactly are we asking them to fund? Be specific about the activity, not the theme.
4. What evidence do we have that this works?
5. What information is missing, and who owns getting it?
6. What does the intervention cost, and what drives that cost?
7. What would additional funding enable, in units the funder cares about?
8. Can the intervention scale, and what would break first if it did?
9. Who would ultimately deliver this at national scale, and who would pay for it?

## The figures table

> **Fill this in before the workshop.** The lines are deliberately blank. Real figures must come from Finance and Programmes. Running this exercise on invented numbers teaches a habit we do not want.

| Line | Figure |
|---|---|
| Programme budget for the year | PKR ________ |
| Children reached | ________ |
| Schools covered | ________ |
| Teachers trained | ________ |
| Direct costs | PKR ________ |
| Indirect costs | PKR ________ |
| Cost per child | Calculate |
| Cost per school | Calculate |
| Cost per outcome | What else would you need to know to calculate this? |

**Follow-up questions for the lead to ask:**

- If our cost per child is this figure, is that good? What would you need to know before answering?
- What happens to cost per child if we double the number of schools? Does it fall, and why?
- If the donor funds only half, what do we cut, and does the programme still work?

# Part 9. Donor role-play question bank

The lead plays the donor. Use these in escalating order.

**Opening**

- Tell me about Taleemabad.
- Why Taleemabad and not one of the other education organisations in Pakistan?

**Evidence**

- How do you know this works?
- What is the strongest evidence you have, and how was it collected?
- What does your evidence not show?

**Economics**

- What is your cost per child?
- Why does this cost so much?
- How does that compare to alternative approaches?

**Judgement**

- Why should I fund this rather than something else in my portfolio?
- What would another two hundred thousand dollars actually achieve?
- What would you do with unrestricted funding that you cannot do with restricted funding?

**Risk and future**

- What are the biggest risks to this programme?
- What happens when my grant ends?
- Who delivers this at national scale, and who pays for it?
- What has gone wrong in this programme before, and what did you change?

> **Debrief question for the lead.** Which question was hardest, and what would we need in order to answer it properly next time? That answer is a development action, not a criticism.

# Part 11. The 30 to 60 day application plan

A workshop that ends in agreement changes nothing. This plan is how it becomes work. The lead should put these on the calendar before the session ends.

## Days 1 to 15

| Action | Owner | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Close the "things we must learn" list | Both, led by workshop lead | Every item answered and written down once, so nobody researches it twice |
| Build the shared funder pipeline with all agreed fields | Workshop lead | One live pipeline, reviewed weekly |
| Score the top ten prospects on the Donor Fit Score | Track A | Ten scored prospects and a call on each: pursue, cultivate, monitor, decline |
| Build a compliance matrix from one real request for proposals | Track B | One matrix, plus a list of information gaps with owners |
| Confirm the core financial figures with Finance | Both | An agreed one page fact sheet of costs and unit costs we are allowed to quote |

## Days 16 to 30

| Action | Owner | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder map for the top three priority funders | Track A | Named people, what each cares about, the access route, the next action |
| Draft one concept note end to end | Track B | A concept note reviewed by the workshop lead and Programmes |
| Agree the evidence we can and cannot claim | Both, with Programmes | A one page evidence sheet, including what we do not yet know |
| First weekly pipeline review | Workshop lead | Pipeline accurate, next actions dated |

## Days 31 to 60

| Action | Owner | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Hold at least two discovery conversations | Track A | Written notes on what each funder actually cares about, in their words |
| Manage one live submission to deadline | Track B | Submitted without a final week scramble, with a post-submission review |
| Build the stewardship calendar for existing funders | Both | Scheduled reports, updates, and one contact that is not an ask |
| Repeat the capability matrix | Everyone | Scores compared against day one, with evidence for any change |

> **The measurement question.** At day sixty, the question is not "did the team enjoy the workshop." It is: what observable behaviour tells us this capability improved, and what actual piece of work demonstrates it? If neither can be answered for a capability, that capability did not develop and needs a different approach.
