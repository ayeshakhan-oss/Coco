## Where this came from

This came out of P&C's work with the fundraising theme. It pulls together three things: what we have picked up from conversations with the team, a look at how fundraising roles are written in our sector, and a read of what several of the funders we care about publish about how they assess organisations, specifically Mulago, Vitol, GiveWell and DIV.

It is a working document rather than a policy. Most of it is P&C structuring external research against what we understand about our own context. The fundraising team knows the funders and the practice better than we do, so parts of this are here to be corrected rather than adopted.

It helps to know which of three things you are reading at any point:

- what external research or a funder's own published material says
- what we are proposing for our team
- what the fundraising team still needs to validate, correct or fill in

Where the document needs a Taleemabad figure, result or example that we do not have confirmed, it says **[To be added/validated with Fundraising / Finance / Programmes]** rather than using an invented example. There are a fair number of these. That is deliberate, and closing them is part of the work.

This document uses roles rather than individual names. The Senior Manager Fundraising would run the workshop. The two team members are referred to as Track A and Track B where the proposed focus areas differ.

There is a shorter facilitator guide and a deck outline that come from this document. Those are for running the session. This one is the reference behind them.

# Part 1. How fundraising moves

One thing we want to avoid is thinking of fundraising as mainly proposal writing. Proposal development is one part of the overall process, and by the time we are writing, quite a lot has usually already been decided by the work that came before it.

The sequence we are proposing as our shared language:

**Research → Qualify → Cultivate → Design the Ask → Proposal → Budget → Due Diligence → Close → Deliver and Report → Steward → Renew**

This is not a Taleemabad process today, it is a way of describing the work so that everyone means the same thing by the same word. If someone says we are at cultivation with a funder, we want the rest of the team to know what has happened and what has not.

For each stage it would be useful to agree four things:

- what we are trying to achieve at this stage
- what should come out of it
- who owns it
- what good work looks like

We have not filled those in. That is a conversation for the fundraising team, and working through it together is probably more useful than P&C proposing answers.

Some places where the process tends to break down, based on the external material and general practice rather than on anything we have diagnosed at Taleemabad:

| Stage | What can go wrong |
|---|---|
| Research | Collecting a long list of funders without understanding any of them properly |
| Qualify | Spending weeks on a funder who was never a realistic fit |
| Cultivate | Pitching before we understand what the funder is actually looking for |
| Proposal | Writing what we want to say rather than answering what was asked |
| Budget | Presenting a number the fundraiser cannot explain |
| Due diligence | Being asked for evidence or policies we have not prepared |
| Report | Treating the work as finished once the money arrives |
| Renew | Going quiet, then getting back in touch when we need funding again |

**Open question for the fundraising team:** which of these are actually live risks for us right now, and which are not worth spending workshop time on?

# Part 2. Questions we want the team to be able to answer

These are the questions we would like both team members to be able to answer about any Taleemabad programme, without reading from a deck.

1. Who should fund this?
2. Why are we a fit for them?
3. What exactly are we asking them to fund?
4. What problem are we solving?
5. Does our solution work?
6. How do we know?
7. What does it cost?
8. Can it scale?
9. What would additional funding enable?
10. Why Taleemabad?
11. How do we build and maintain that relationship?

A useful way to use these in the session is to ask them cold and write down what we cannot answer. That list is more honest than a self-assessment, and it tells us what to work on. It may also turn up things that are gaps in what the organisation has documented rather than gaps in the team's knowledge, which is worth separating.

# Part 3. The capabilities we want to build

Twelve areas. They are not equally urgent, and the Senior Manager Fundraising may want to reorder or drop some. For each one we have tried to say what we would expect to see in practice rather than give a definition.

## 1. Understanding our own organisation

Before anyone researches a donor, they should be able to explain Taleemabad without notes: what we do, who we serve, where we work, what we have achieved, who funds us now, what we are scaling, and what makes us different from other education organisations in Pakistan.

Two separate things get confused here. Explaining what Taleemabad is, and explaining why someone should fund us, are different answers. It is worth practising them separately.

We should also be clear about which claims we can defend and which we cannot. That depends on what Programmes and the evidence team can actually stand behind.

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising / Programmes: current reach figures, the outcomes we are comfortable quoting externally, and current funders we can name.]**

## 2. Donor research

For any funder we are looking at, we would want the team to be able to say what they fund, where they fund, roughly what size of grant they give, what they have funded before, what their published strategy tells us, how organisations actually get in front of them, who the relevant people are, and whether we have any existing connection.

When we review this work, it would be better to focus on the quality of the recommendation than on the amount of information collected. A short note that reaches a defensible conclusion is more useful than a long one that does not.

## 3. Donor qualification

Research on its own does not tell us whether to pursue someone. We are proposing a simple scoring approach so the decision is explicit rather than instinctive. Score each dimension 1 to 5:

| Dimension | The question |
|---|---|
| Mission fit | Do they fund the problem we work on? |
| Geography fit | Do they fund Pakistan, and the regions we work in? |
| Programme fit | Do our programmes match their stated priorities? |
| Evidence fit | Do we have the kind of evidence they expect? |
| Scale fit | Does our scale ambition match theirs? |
| Ticket size | Would the likely grant be meaningful for us? |
| Relationship | Do we have access, or a route in through someone? |
| Timing | Is there something live now? |
| Effort | Is the opportunity worth the work it would take? |

The output would be one of four calls: pursue, cultivate, monitor, or decline. Recording why we declined matters, because the same funder will come up again in six months and someone will have to answer the question a second time.

This is our proposal, not established practice. The Senior Manager Fundraising may already have a way of making this call, in which case we should use that.

## 4. Stakeholder mapping

Funding decisions are made by people, not institutions. For each priority funder we would want to know who decides, who influences, who inside the organisation might champion us, who controls access, and who we might already know who could connect us.

The output of a mapping exercise should be a next action with a name against it, not a diagram.

This one matters more for some funders than others. Vitol is the clearest case in our current set, for reasons covered in Part 6.

## 5. Donor engagement and listening

Cultivation is the work of understanding a funder and building trust before making an ask. The habit we want is entering a first conversation to learn rather than to pitch, and coming out able to describe what the funder cares about in their own words.

Some questions worth practising:

- What priorities matter most to your foundation at the moment?
- What kinds of education investments are you most interested in?
- What evidence do you look at when you assess an organisation?
- What usually stops you from funding an organisation?

The role-play in the workshop is built around this. The rule we are suggesting is no pitching for the first five minutes.

## 6. Storytelling and pitching

A funding case needs the human story and the evidence and the economics together. A story on its own does not survive a serious donor conversation, and a set of numbers on its own does not give anyone a reason to care.

A structure we could use:

| Element | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Problem | What is happening? |
| People | Who does it affect? |
| Stakes | Why does it matter? |
| Intervention | What are we doing about it? |
| Evidence | What shows that it works? |
| Ask | What would this funder's money do? |
| Scale | What happens if this grows? |

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising: whether we already have an agreed case for support or standard pitch, in which case we should teach that rather than a generic structure.]**

## 7. Proposal and grant development

This is the part the team probably already associates with fundraising, so the useful thing is where it sits in the sequence rather than the mechanics.

A practical way to work through it is to take a real request for proposals and go through four steps: mark every requirement in the document, build a compliance matrix showing requirement, where we answer it, who owns it and its status, write a one page skeleton, and then list what information is missing.

That last step is the one we would push on. Saying "I need Finance to confirm this figure" or "I need Programmes to give me this result" is the right answer. Filling the gap with something plausible is not.

Content areas to build over time: eligibility, concept notes, problem statements, theory of change, objectives, activities, outputs, outcomes, indicators, implementation plan, evidence, risk, sustainability, scale, and the ask itself. We do not need to cover all of these in one session.

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising: a real RFP we can use, ideally one we are actually considering.]**

## 8. Financial literacy

Covered in more detail in Part 5. The short version is that fundraisers are not expected to replace Finance, but they should understand the numbers well enough to explain the funding case and to know when Finance needs to step in.

## 9. Evidence and M&E literacy

The team should understand the evidence they are presenting, including its limits. Output, outcome and impact are different things. Baseline and endline, indicators, theory of change, comparison groups and attribution are worth understanding at a working level.

A practical exercise: take one result we quote to funders and trace it back. Where did the number come from, what was measured, over what period, and compared to what.

Being able to say "we do not have that evidence yet" is part of the capability, not a failure of it.

**[To be added/validated with Programmes: which of our results have been externally evaluated, and which are internal monitoring data.]**

## 10. Cost-effectiveness and scale

Cost-effectiveness means impact relative to cost, and it only means something in comparison to alternatives. If someone asks whether a given cost per child is good, the honest first answer is that we cannot say yet, and then: what outcome are we achieving, what else could achieve it, what does that cost, and what difference did we actually make compared to doing nothing.

Scale is the other half. If our work succeeds, someone has to run it at national level and someone has to pay for it. Mulago and DIV both press on this, so it is worth having a considered answer rather than assembling one during an application.

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising / Programmes: our current thinking on who delivers at scale and who pays, particularly on the government side.]**

## 11. Pipeline management

We are proposing one shared pipeline with, at a minimum: donor, opportunity, owner, estimated value, stage, probability, deadline, latest interaction, next action, next action date, proposal status, decision, and reporting deadline.

The pipeline will only be useful if we keep it current, particularly the next action, owner, expected value and status of each opportunity. A weekly review is probably the simplest way to hold that.

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising: whether a pipeline already exists and in what form. If it does, we should improve it rather than start another one.]**

## 12. Donor stewardship and ethics

Stewardship is everything after the money arrives: delivering, communicating, reporting, showing what happened, staying in contact, and opening the renewal conversation. The simplest test is whether a funder hears from us when we are not asking for something.

On ethics, three things we would want to hold to regardless of pressure:

- we do not overstate impact to win funding
- we do not use a statistic we cannot source and defend
- we do not promise a use of funds that Programmes or Finance cannot deliver

These are consistent with the professional standards published by AFP, which cover honesty, transparency, honouring donor intent, and reporting costs and results accurately.

# Part 4. How the two roles could develop

This is a proposal, and it needs the Senior Manager Fundraising's judgement more than any other section. We are not proposing to split the work permanently, only to build depth somewhere while both people learn the whole cycle.

**Track A, donor intelligence and relationships.** Donor and prospect research, qualification, stakeholder mapping, cultivation and discovery conversations, and follow-up discipline.

**Track B, opportunity and proposal development.** Opportunity and RFP analysis, concept notes and problem statements, proposal writing and coordination with Programmes and Finance, evidence integration, and submission management.

**Shared regardless of focus.** Financial literacy, evidence literacy, storytelling, pipeline discipline, stewardship and ethics.

Two things to be careful about. First, these are primary areas, not exclusive ones, and neither person should be able to opt out of a stage because it sits in the other track. Second, this split is based on P&C's reading of the roles and the conversations we have had, not on a performance assessment. The Senior Manager Fundraising should confirm whether it matches how the team is actually meant to work, and whether the team members' own interests point somewhere different.

Individual development plans sit outside this document and would be agreed separately.

# Part 5. The financial side

Budgeting is where credibility tends to be lost in donor conversations, and it is the area where a fundraiser most often has to hold a position under questioning. Candid's material on proposal budgets is a good grounding, particularly on staff allocation, indirect costs and in-kind contributions.

Concepts the team should be comfortable with:

| Concept | Why it matters in a donor conversation |
|---|---|
| Programme cost | The total behind any ask |
| Direct costs | What a donor can see themselves funding |
| Indirect costs | The overhead question, which we need to be able to justify |
| Fixed and variable costs | Explains what happens to cost as volume changes |
| Cost per child | The unit most donors think in |
| Cost per school or teacher | Where it is the more meaningful unit for that programme |
| Cost per outcome | Where we have enough data to calculate it |
| Cost drivers | What actually moves the total |
| Budget assumptions | Why a number is what it is |
| Restricted and unrestricted funding | What the money can and cannot be used for |
| Co-funding | Who else is in, and what that signals to a new funder |
| Funding gap | The honest size of what is still needed |
| In-kind contribution | Value that does not come as cash |
| Cost recovery | Whether we are covering our own costs |
| Budget variance | The gap between plan and actual, which reporting will surface |

Questions a donor is likely to ask, and which the team should be able to answer or route to Finance:

- Why does this programme cost this much?
- What is our cost per child?
- What would another PKR 10M enable?
- What happens if we receive only half of the ask?
- What changes financially if we scale? Does unit cost fall, and why?
- Which of these costs are direct and which are indirect?
- Which numbers do we need Finance to validate before we talk to a donor?

Fundraisers are not expected to replace Finance. They should understand the numbers well enough to explain the funding case and to recognise when Finance needs to be brought in. "I will confirm that with Finance and come back to you today" is a reasonable answer in a donor meeting. A guess is not.

**[To be added/validated with Finance: one real programme budget we can use in the workshop, and an agreed set of unit costs the team is allowed to quote externally.]**

# Part 6. What the funders we care about actually look for

This section is external research. It is what these four funders publish about their own assessment, summarised. It is not a description of our relationship with any of them, and anything that does not match the fundraising team's own experience should be corrected.

| Funder | What they say they assess | What that could mean for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Mulago Foundation** | Whether a model is good enough, big enough, simple enough and cheap enough to scale. Good enough covers consequential impact and quality of evidence. Cheap enough asks whether the eventual payer can and will actually pay for it. | We would need a clear answer on who delivers at scale and who pays, not only a description of the programme. |
| **GiveWell** | Evidence of effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, room for more funding, and transparency. | We would need to be able to say what additional money specifically enables, rather than that we need it. |
| **DIV Fund** | Evidence of impact, cost-effectiveness, and a credible path to durable scale. Looks at unit cost, per-person impact, comparison against alternative approaches, theory of change, and whether the intervention can realistically reach scale. Later stage funding expects evidence that costs and delivery hold up at scale. | Our economics and our comparison against alternatives would need to be prepared in advance. |
| **Vitol Foundation** | Funds Early Childhood Care and Education, Adolescent Health, Humanitarian Emergencies, and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene. Looks for work that strengthens performance, access and inclusion, innovation, and access to financing, delivered within local systems rather than around them. Does not accept unsolicited applications: organisations are invited to apply by a programme manager. Offers restricted funding within project budgets or fully unrestricted support, and describes itself as a flexible funder able to bridge gaps as organisations move toward scale. Process typically takes three to six months. | With no open application round, the route in matters more than the strength of a written application. For Vitol, the work is relationship and access, not proposal writing. |

Two ideas from this material that are worth teaching explicitly.

**Room for more funding.** GiveWell does not only ask whether an organisation is good, it asks what additional money would actually enable, and it has paused funding for organisations it rated well where it judged they could not productively use more at that point. The practical implication for us is that saying we need a given amount is weaker than saying what the next tranche adds: how many more schools, teachers and children, and what we expect to come out of it.

**Who delivers and who pays at scale.** If the intervention works, someone eventually runs it nationally and someone funds it, whether that is government, philanthropy, users or a large aid actor. Both Mulago and DIV push on this and the question is harder than it sounds.

**[To be added/validated with Fundraising: our current status with each of these four, and whether there are others that should be on this list instead.]**

A fuller version of this is in the separate facilitator guide. In summary, we are proposing roughly two and a half hours, mostly practical, structured as:

1. How fundraising moves, and where we spend our time now (15 min)
2. How well do we know Taleemabad (20 min)

7. What happens after the workshop (10 min)

# Part 8. Practice exercises

These sit behind the workshop. Where possible they should use real Taleemabad material rather than invented cases.

## Building a funding case

Take one real Taleemabad intervention and work through:

1. Who should fund this, and why them specifically?
2. Why are we a fit, in their own stated terms?
3. What exactly are we asking them to fund? The activity, not the theme.
4. What evidence do we have that it works?
5. What is missing, and who owns getting it?
6. What does it cost, and what drives that cost?
7. What would additional funding enable, in units the funder cares about?
8. Can it scale, and what would come under strain first?
9. Who would deliver it at national scale, and who would pay?

## Donor conversation

The facilitator plays the donor. Questions to draw on:

- Tell me about Taleemabad. Why you and not another education organisation here?
- How do you know this works? What is your strongest evidence and how was it collected? What does it not show?
- What is your cost per child? Why does it cost this much? How does that compare to other approaches?
- Why should I fund this rather than something else in my portfolio?
- What would another USD 200,000 achieve?
- What would you do with unrestricted funding that you cannot do with restricted?
- What are the main risks? What happens when my grant ends? Who takes this to scale and who pays?

Worth debriefing afterwards on which question was hardest and what we would need in order to answer it properly. That points at real work rather than at a personal weakness.

# Part 10. Turning it into work

The workshop is not the development plan. What would make the difference is the assignments that follow it, with the Senior Manager Fundraising reviewing the output.

Some things we could put in the first month:

- close out the list of questions the team could not answer in the session, so nobody researches the same thing twice
- set up or tidy the shared pipeline, with a weekly review
- Track A scores our top prospects on the qualification dimensions and makes a call on each
- Track B builds a compliance matrix from a real RFP and lists the information gaps
- both, with Finance: agree the cost figures and unit costs we are allowed to quote

And in the second month:

- Track A maps the people around our two or three priority funders and holds at least one discovery conversation, written up afterwards
- Track B drafts a concept note end to end and manages one live submission to deadline
- both, with Programmes: agree what our evidence supports and what it does not
- set up the stewardship calendar for existing funders, including at least one contact that is not an ask

At 30 to 60 days the question worth asking is not whether the workshop was useful. It is what someone can now do that they could not before, and what piece of work shows it. If neither the manager nor the person can point to something, the development needs a different approach.

Individual development plans would be agreed separately between the Senior Manager Fundraising and each team member.
