# How Zeest Interviews

## Question Patterns & Emphases — Growth Manager Case-Study Debriefs

**Prepared for:** Ayesha Khan, People & Culture  
**Interviewer analysed:** Zeest Hassan Qureshi  
**Evidence base:** 3 case-study debrief transcripts — Salman Tariq (10 Aug, 72 min) · Muhammad Waqas (11 Aug, 61 min) · Ahmad Wajahat Sheikh (12 Aug, 84 min)  
**Purpose:** Internal reference — how Zeest runs a Growth interview, what she is testing for, and a reusable question bank

> **Read this first — the limits of this document**
>
> Three transcripts, all the same role family (Growth Manager, government-facing) and all the same stage (post-case-study debrief). First, **all three transcripts were cut off before the call ended** — Salman at roughly 1:00 of 72 minutes, Waqas at roughly 52:00 of 61, Ahmad at 1:19:39 of 84 — so her closing minutes are never captured. ## The one-paragraph summary

Zeest runs a **conversational stress test, not a q-and-a**. She discloses her method at the top, checks the candidate understands the job, then spends the bulk of the hour pushing a small number of **real, current Taleemabad situations — the same ones, reused across all three candidates** — progressively harder until the candidate either produces a concrete move or runs out of road. She is not testing knowledge of education, government or AI; she supplies all of that herself, generously. She is testing **whether this person can get into a room they have no right to be in, and change the mind of the person sitting in it.** She says so out loud in all three calls. Everything else — access, contacts, translation of incentives, persistence, ethics — is a component of that one thing.

## What repeats across all three calls

The frequency column is the most useful thing in this document: it separates her fixed method from her improvisation. Anything marked 3/3 should be written into the Growth interview standard.

| Move | Frequency | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| “What does a day in this role look like?” as the first substantive question | **3 / 3** | Asked before any praise or correction, so the answer is uncoached |
| Discloses her own interview style before using it | **3 / 3** | “dynamic”, “things I’ll throw at you in the moment”, warns a thought experiment is coming |
| Access question: who would you call — and if nobody, how do you get in? | **3 / 3** | Her single most-pressed line of questioning |
| The PECTA blocked-pilot scenario (SED ruling, minister unreachable, Kashmir) | **3 / 3** | Verbatim reuse — role-play for Waqas and Ahmad, pressure test for Salman |
| Live role-play in which she plays the counterpart | **3 / 3** | PECTA CEO (×2) and Sindh Education Minister Sardar Shah (×1) |
| Bribe / kickback question | **3 / 3** | 1% (Salman), 3% of project cost (Waqas), 2% (Ahmad) |
| Relationship portability — would those contacts still take your call? | **3 / 3** | Narrowed to individuals and to cold opens in each call |
| Names “charm the room” as the heart of the role | **3 / 3** | Stated explicitly to each candidate, in near-identical words |
| Escalation ladder — closes each escape route until only a decision remains | **3 / 3** | Sharpest in the Waqas renewal sequence (19:13–22:10) |

# PART 1 — What Zeest Is Actually Emphasising

Ranked by airtime, by pressure applied, and by what she says explicitly.

### 1. Change the mind in the room *(3 / 3 · her stated core)*

This is the emphasis everything else serves, and she names it in every call in almost the same words. It is also the axis on which she pushed back hardest on a candidate.

> **To Ahmad, closing the role-play (1:11:19):**
>
> “This is probably the gist of the role itself, because building relationships and sustaining them and manipulating conversations for your organization’s incentive is the heart of the role.”
>
> **To Waqas, explaining why the Lahore hire exists (35:50):**
>
> “There will be obstacles… but there has to be somebody who is able to go in the room and **charm these government stakeholders** to make sure that they’re able to deliver.”
>
> **To Salman, challenging his answers to his face (30:43):**
>
> “A lot of your answers… are based about diagnosing the problem, figuring out what went wrong… In growth, a lot of that is already being done by AI for us. And what we end up doing actually in the room is making sure that… that person is fully charmed and really excited to work with us… What I’m just trying to do is change their mind in the room. And that’s where the key is within this role.”
>

**What this means for scoring:** research, frameworks and diagnosis are table stakes and she treats them as such. A candidate who answers a blocked-deal scenario with “first I would understand why” is answering the wrong question, and she will say so.

### 2. Access — and the end-run when the door is shut *(3 / 3 · most pressure)*

Her signature question is never “do you have contacts”. It is the two-part version: **who would you call, and if you know nobody, how do you manufacture a way in.** She asks it in all three calls and escalates until she gets a name or a mechanism.

- To Waqas (9:30): “you picked PITB as the entry point. I’m assuming you don’t have an existing relationship with someone in PITB. So how would you approach that? And how would you try to **get in the room**?” — then, when he drifted into pipeline theory, she re-framed it harder: “regardless of all of that, I’m wondering how you would push to get yourself in the room. Let’s say… this is the first time Taleemabad is entering Punjab.”
- To Ahmad (41:54): “**A**, who would you call if you know someone there? And if you don’t, how would you approach them? And then how would you try to make an end[-]r[un] to get to know someone there? And **B**, how would you have that conversation?”
- To Salman (12:33): “How would you… [make] a relationship go from just somebody you know to somebody who gives you access? **A**, that, and **B**, how would you get access when you’re outside of these structures, not inside?”
She rewards a **named mechanism**, not willingness — and in the Waqas call she went as far as supplying her own answer, which is the clearest statement of the standard she holds candidates to:

> **Zeest to Waqas, endorsing his instinct to camp outside the office (15:43):**
>
> “You are correct in terms of assuming that spending time in front of government offices is absolutely key. You have to just **leave your self-respect and pride at home** sometimes and just go there and be there and sit with them and have 15 cups of tea if that’s what it takes. But that’s how you push the paper forward.”
>

Ahmad named two Muslim Hands executives and the Director Programs seat at PEF; Salman named Khurram Zafar and generalised it into pressure groups; Waqas described physically going to the deputy director’s office because it was on his way home. All three landed, and she praised each explicitly — “the idea about approaching a side channel and not giving up is very important” (Ahmad, 1:00:38); “the idea behind pressure groups… is very important” (Salman, 19:28).

### 3. Translate our incentive into their mandate *(3 / 3 · most rewarded)*

The candidates’ strongest moments were all versions of this move, and she supplies her own worked example unprompted — which tells you it is a live problem for the team, not a hypothetical.

> **Zeest’s own example, given to Salman (30:43):**
>
> “I go to Balochistan and they say, we’re not focused on AI right now, we’re only focused on teacher training. I say, okay. And now I have to figure out how to market my product in a way that to them, it’s teacher training — even though technically it’s still AI — and change the conversation in that way. And all of that is happening without diagnosing why they don’t want AI, because that’s irrelevant, frankly.”
>

Salman had independently framed his own job the same way — “translate what you are doing… to their vocabulary” (2:00) — and she thanked him for the answer and the example. Waqas produced the equivalent inside the role-play, pitching Rumi on WhatsApp ubiquity rather than on AI (“even if you go into the remote areas, they know what WhatsApp is”). Ahmad got credit for the hierarchy version of it — knowing not to go over the secretary’s head.

### 4. Are the relationships portable, or venue-specific? *(3 / 3 · the trap)*

Having accepted that a candidate has contacts, she immediately tests whether the *skill* travels or whether it is an artefact of one long tenure. The Waqas version is the sharpest of the three because it asks the uncomfortable half out loud.

> **To Waqas (48:51):**
>
> “If tomorrow you have to call someone up in any of those — experiences, fellowships, grants, all the people you met, the network you’ve built — how would those conversations go? And **would all of them pick up your call?** Or do you think some organizations or some experiences were where people would pick up your call, but other people won’t?”
>
> **To Salman (27:21):**
>
> “Do you think that’s true of any organization you’ve worked with in the past? Not even just worked at, but worked with or collaborated with or volunteered with… or do you think that PIT[B] is special because you spent so much time there?”
>
> **To Ahmad (48:48):**
>
> “Could you go back to any organization… in the last maybe five, six years… and be able to pick up any contact and leverage that political capital or social capital and convert it for today? I’m just wondering how you sustain relationships.”
>

She then narrows from organisational to **individual**, and from warm to **cold** — to Salman (51:20): “give me an example of a **cold open** with any particular person who turned out to be influential, who you then sustained a relationship with, who you could ask very comfortably.” This is the question that separates a well-connected incumbent from a genuine relationship-builder. It produced the single best answer in any of the three calls (Salman’s LinkedIn campaign that brought DigitalOcean, Notion and Miro startup credits into Pakistan).

### 5. Persistence, and the refusal of abstraction *(3 / 3)*

Her escalation pattern is consistent: she removes each escape route until only a decision remains. This is the clearest scoring instrument she has, and the Waqas renewal sequence is the textbook example — five consecutive narrowings in three minutes.

| Candidate’s escape route | Zeest’s closing of it |
|---|---|
| “I’d ask for a no-cost extension” | “We have enough money to pay all of the coaches for the next month. After that… we will have to lay people off… you no longer have the room to go towards them with an extension request.” (Waqas, 19:13) |
| “I’d work towards the renewal” | “It’s 1st June. You need to get this renewal done, otherwise you have to lay off 50 people. What would you do? **I mean, really, what would you do?**” (20:42) |
| “We’d give it our best” | “I’m going to push this a little more… What would you **actually** do when you say you do your best?… knowing that you have 30 days to deliver.” (21:32) |
| “I’d understand their perspective first” | “They don’t want to answer that. They’re like, no, we don’t [want] AI. **End of story.**” (Salman, 32:58) |
| “I’d build the impact case” | “So that’s theoretical… the reason it was approved in the first place was because they already knew it worked… So all of these things are secondary.” (Salman, 22:00) |
| “I’d find out what changed” | “What if you can’t find out the reason? Then you just have to figure out how to renew it, but you don’t know why it went wrong.” (Salman, 23:48) |
| A general principle instead of an action | “So what do you do? **And I’m sure you know this**, so what do you do?” (Ahmad, 56:03) |
| Narrating the pitch in third person | “I’m wondering if you can have this conversation, assuming that I am the person you’re talking to, rather than them, they, etc. **Just talk to me.**” (Salman, 42:31) |

She names resilience as the quality being measured — “that shows resilience, which I think I really appreciate in this particular role” (Ahmad, 1:01:26) — and Waqas independently named it back to her as the skill he would carry over from NGO work.

### 6. The ethics floor *(3 / 3 · hard gate)*

Zeest puts a kickback demand to every candidate, in local, unmistakable terms, and separates hospitality from corruption herself so the candidate cannot hide in the ambiguity.

> **To Waqas (42:09), then sharpening it when he answered about chai-pani:**
>
> “So would you say yes to a bribe if it meets your end goal?” … “When I say bribe, I mean like **3% of the entire project cost**. I’m talking about high-level bribes.”
>
> **And she states the purpose of the question outright (43:13):**
>
> “The reason I’m trying to understand this is… how do we do and achieve relationship goals **without sacrificing our ethics**, and how far you can go. So that’s kind of what I’m trying to assess.”
>
> **To Salman (18:50):**
>
> “Let’s say they say, we basically want 1% of all potential scales should go directly into the minister’s pocket — then what [do] we [do]?”
>
> **To Ahmad (1:14:27):**
>
> “Let’s say you have a green light for a new pilot or for a paid scale, but they say that it will [cost] 2%… How do you manage that conversation?”
>

**The three answers diverged sharply, which is exactly what a good gate does.** Salman refused immediately on reputational grounds (“that would create an unethical position that would weaken the position of Taleemabad… if you do this once, it would weaken the credi[bility]”). Ahmad described the practice as endemic and refusal as costly — “you can refuse, but then there are many consequences” — and defaulted to waiting for the next secretary. Waqas deferred the decision upward: “I will actually have to discuss that with my administration… if they say you can do that, then I will do it”, and when pressed, “if we want it that bad, then I might do it.” Zeest followed up rather than letting it sit — “so if your administration gives you the green light, you’ll just go for it?”

She stated Taleemabad’s own position **only in Ahmad’s call** (1:17:11): “Taleemabad is a very strict values company… we [might] take them to dinner and take [them to] lunch. When it comes to skimming off the top, we are very strict. We will give up our entire payment… But we don’t [pay].” Asking first and disclosing second is the right order — it stops the candidate reverse-engineering the approved answer — but it was only done once. See Part 4.

### 7. Does the candidate own the number, or the narrative? *(1 / 3 · worth keeping)*

The best single question in the three transcripts, asked of Waqas about a headline CV figure (5:35):

> “You mentioned in your CV that you were able to contribute 8.7 million in revenue when you were at ScienceFuse. Can you walk me through **one deal inside that number** that you took start to end — what did they pay for, how did you convince them to pay, [and] things that almost killed the deal and how you managed that?”
>

It is unbluffable: a candidate who inherited a number cannot narrate a single deal through it. Waqas passed — he named the National Catholic Education Commission, the 1.6 million first-year figure, how a co-working-space friend introduced him, the two months of “we’re busy, this isn’t our priority”, and the module structure he built. Recommend this becomes standard for any revenue-carrying CV.

### 8. Is the case study actually theirs? *(1 / 3 · highest-value catch)*

She asked Waqas to define a term he had used in his own submission (24:04): “you’re mentioning that either you’ll do a fresh multi-year renewal, or you will go for **regularization**. I’m trying to understand what you understand when you say regularization.”

> **His answer:**
>
> “This is something I would say **AI must have written**, because I cannot justify regularization… I will be very honest. I cannot.”
>

She accepted the honesty without penalty theatre (“that’s fine”) and then **taught him the concept** — that regularization means the programme becomes a line item in the provincial or federal budget rather than a grant renewed every few years, that World Bank or UNICEF money is “a temporary band-aid”, and that it takes two to two and a half years to get there. Two lessons for the team: **asking a candidate to define their own jargon is the cheapest AI-detection tool available**, and her instinct to teach rather than trap is why candidates keep being honest with her.

### 9. Do the skills transfer — including from private life? *(2 / 3)*

To Waqas (38:23), who has NGO and donor experience but none with government: “How would you translate those skills to working with the government?… have you ever tried to build such relationships? **And it doesn’t have to be necessary in a working capacity** — maybe you would navigate [red] tape to get some stuff done in your own life. You mentioned doing things after your father passed away.” Accepting non-work evidence is both humane and sound: for a candidate crossing sectors, the transferable proof often sits outside their CV. Ahmad got the same test in its funding form — “have you actually carried out that funnel?” from unfunded to funded — and Salman in its commercial form — “have you moved a free pilot or proof of concept to a converted deal?”

Note her handling when Salman answered honestly that revenue conversion was never his mandate: she **widened the credit** rather than marking him down — “creating any kind of leverage and moving that towards the benefit of an organization would count in the same category… as converting something free-to-paid.” She rewards the honest no; she does not reward invention. Salman had said “I don’t want to make things up.”

### 10. Convening design and stakeholder assembly *(1 / 3)*

Tested properly only in the Waqas call (44:00): “let’s go to the part of your study where you designed a room… how would you bring all of these stakeholders in one room if you had two months to organize the event?… It’s 30 days, 60 days. How would you do it?” When he proposed working bottom-up to secure a VIP list, she challenged the instinct and converted it into an on-the-spot cold call:

> **Zeest (46:32), interrupting:**
>
> “Can I pause you? Why do you think [you have to] start small? Let’s say I give you the number of the secretary of Punjab — I haven’t talked to him, I just got his number. **Call him.** How would you call him and talk to him?”
>

Worth noting because the Growth Manager case study tests convening design on paper (Assignment 2) and only one of three debriefs pressure-tested it out loud.

### 11. Appetite for the job as it actually is *(1 / 3)*

Late in Salman’s call she tested fit rather than capability (58:17): “how do you feel about the fact that we don’t work with the private sector at all? We work primarily with governments — provincial, international, federal?” Asked immediately after he had pitched Taleemabad becoming a startup-ecosystem builder, the question does two jobs: checking he wants the government grind, and checking whether his own ambition points somewhere else.

# PART 2 — Her Techniques, and Why They Work

### 1. She discloses her method before using it

“Let me just walk you through how I generally conduct interviews… **A**, I like to keep things dynamic, I like to keep things moving. And **B**, I like to understand what kind of person you are holistically, not just on paper… Sometimes I have a little thought experiment in [my] role, so I’m just giving you a heads up” (Ahmad, 13:44). To Waqas: “I like to do a bit of dynamic questioning. There’ll be some things that I’ll pick out from your case… but there will also be some things that I’ll **throw at you in the moment**.” Pre-announcing the ambush removes the unfairness without removing the difficulty — nobody can claim they were blindsided, and she gets a cleaner signal. She also owns her own intensity: “I know I get a little in your face sometimes” (Waqas, 37:53).

### 2. She reuses the same scenarios across candidates

The **PECTA blocked pilot** appears in all three interviews with the same components: the SED policy revision, the CEO losing approval authority, the minister unreachable because Punjab is consumed by the Kashmir issue and the re-election. Waqas and Ahmad got it as a role-play; Salman got it as a pressure test on his own framework. The kickback question and the “day in this role” opener are equally fixed. **Reusing a fixed scenario is what makes candidates comparable, and it is the most valuable thing in her method for the rest of the team to copy.**

### 3. She gives away real context generously

She hands over material most interviewers would guard: Rumi’s full mechanics (an AI teacher co-pilot running entirely on WhatsApp — lesson plans, voice-note capture of a live class, scoring against the government rubric, feedback on closed versus open-ended questioning, attendance, reading assessments); the fact that Rumi’s codebase is **open source**, cloned by developers in India and adaptable to agriculture, health records and mental-health triage; the Prevail-funded Rawalpindi pilot sanctioned by Punjab; the Beth/PEF pilot; the PECTA collapse; deployments in Tanzania, Kenya, Palestine and the West Bank; the shift away from private-sector B2B2G; the 2030 goal of 350 million children.

In the Waqas call she went further — she **stopped the role-play** on discovering he did not know the product, taught him Rumi properly, and restarted. His reaction is the argument for doing it: “if I didn’t have the context, I would have said maybe something which I had no idea about… it actually gave me an idea on-spot.” This makes the interview a **thinking test rather than a research test** — nobody wins it by having read the website — and doubles as a sell to a senior candidate.

### 4. She verifies claims with insider knowledge

“I’ve worked at PITB as well, so I do have understanding of the organization. So is Plan 9 still a part of PITB, or has it been replaced by the incubation wing?” (Salman, 5:25). She later drops that Galaxy Lollywood was a Plan 9 startup. Low-cost, high-yield authenticity checks that a candidate embellishing a tenure cannot survive. Worth adopting as a **panel-design principle**: put someone in the room who has actually worked inside the counterpart institution.

### 5. She reflects back before moving on

“So you’re basically saying that there was a lot of effort involved in moving the needle… and you were offering support to the team that sort of executed it. **Am I correct?**” (Ahmad, 27:19). Two functions: it confirms she has followed a long, meandering answer, and it quietly re-states the answer in its weakest honest form — giving the candidate a fair chance to correct the record before she scores it.

### 6. She re-asks the half that got dropped

Ahmad answered why he chose PMIU and skipped how he would get the meeting; she came straight back: “it was how you would go about achieving the success indicator of getting a meeting with the MD of PMIU. Like, how would you go about it?” (31:47). Waqas answered the pipeline question instead of the access question and got the same treatment. **Double-barrelled A/B questions are a deliberate device** — which half a candidate answers, and which half they let slide, is itself signal.

### 7. She plays a hard, informed, sometimes hostile counterpart

As the PECTA CEO facing Waqas she switched into Urdu and played the official openly irritated — *“Taleemabad wale, aap log phir aa gaye hain… policy badal gayi hai, aap baar baar aate hain, abhi hum pilot shuru nahi kar sakte, kya masla hai… aap log samajh nahi rahe hain hamari [baat]”*. When he protested that he had pictured a friendlier, more casual encounter, she corrected the frame in one line:

> **Zeest (31:10):**
>
> “Waqas, this is the entirety of the government we deal with. **This is the baseline.**”
>

As Sindh Education Minister Sardar Shah facing Salman she raised a real minister’s objections in order: accountability (“if you inject them, then teachers will use AI to teach… we don’t know what happens in class”), then budget cannibalisation (“we pay teacher training, we pay PITE… if we put AI, then we will not do our work”), then scale and offline capability. She constrains the scene realistically — ten minutes, mid-meeting, other people in the room — and always offers prep time first, so what she tests is judgment under pressure, not cold-start improvisation.

### 8. She protects the candidate’s dignity throughout

- Power cut mid-answer: “we all live in Pakistan, we understand the limitations… We can also do this at a later time… **It won’t count against you.**” (Ahmad, 27:00) — and to you off-mic beforehand: “I don’t want him to panic.”
- Language: “please feel free to switch languages. **I’m not married to English.**” (Ahmad, 34:38) — after which he gave his strongest answers in Urdu. She code-switches into Urdu herself with Waqas.
- Names: “your name is Ahmed or Wajahat — which should I call you?” (13:30).
- Lets a candidate open their own case study mid-answer: “of course, of course, you can open your case study” (Waqas, 24:15).
- Thanks candidates for tolerating the format: “thank you for going along with me on that experiment”, “I know it’s an unconventional thing to do in an interview.”
- Explains why the debrief exists at all: “we make it a point… because you’ve put in the effort. So it’s important for us to also go through the case study… and figure out what we can pick up” (Salman, 46:58).
This is worth protecting as house style. Salman volunteered that the process itself taught him something — “there are times when you go through a recruitment process and you get to learn something, and this is one of th[ose]” — and Waqas thanked her unprompted for the mid-role-play teaching.

### 9. She tells the candidate her hypothesis about their weakness

Rather than quietly marking Salman down for being diagnosis-heavy, she said it to his face and asked him to answer it (30:43). Higher-risk and higher-yield: the candidate gets one clean chance to disprove the read, and the interviewer avoids scoring a first impression that never got tested.

# PART 3 — Reusable Question Bank

Generalised from what Zeest actually asked, so the wording can be reused for the next Growth or partnerships hire. Items marked **[3/3]** were asked of every candidate and should be treated as standard.

### Opening — role comprehension

- **[3/3]** “What do you think a day in this role looks like?” — asked before any praise or correction.
- Follow with silence. Waqas’s honest “I had no idea… I have never worked with government before, in full disclosure” was more useful than a polished answer would have been.

### Case study — interrogating what they wrote

- Name one specific strength first, then stress it: “you made sure the success indicators were very pointed — [now] how would you secure that success indicator?”
- “Why did you choose **this** arm of government, as opposed to [two named alternatives]?”
- **Define your own jargon:** “you wrote [term]. What do you understand when you say [term]?” — the cheapest AI-detection tool available.
- “Your reflective note was a little brief — I wanted more insight on it.”
- Anchor to a named company value: “you leaned into [value] in your one-pager. How have you done that in your own work — and how would you push that value externally?” Then: “any example you can come up with?”

### CV verification

- “Walk me through **one deal inside that number** — start to end. What did they pay for, how did you convince them, what almost killed it, and how did you manage that?”
- An insider fact-check on a claimed tenure, asked by someone who has worked in that institution.

### Access and end-runs

- **[3/3]** “Who would you call? And if you know nobody — how do you get in the room?”
- “Assume this is the **first time** we are entering [province]. No existing relationship. How do you push yourself into the room?”
- “How do you get access when you are **outside** these structures rather than inside them?”
- “How do you turn somebody you merely know into somebody who gives you access?”
- The cold call, live: “I have the secretary’s number and no relationship. **Call him.** How does that conversation go?”

### Relationship durability — the portability trap

- **[3/3]** “Could you go back to any organisation from the last five or six years, pick up a contact, and convert that capital today?”
- “Would **all** of them pick up your call — or only some? Which ones, and why?”
- “Is that true of every organisation you have worked with — or is [current employer] special because you were there so long?”
- “Give me an example of a **cold open** with someone influential, whom you then sustained, and could comfortably call today.”

### Skill transfer and funnel ownership

- “Your experience is [donor / NGO / private]. How does that translate to government — and **it doesn’t have to be a work example**; navigating bureaucracy in your own life counts.”
- “Have you taken something from **unfunded to funded** — or were you supporting a team that did?”
- “Have you moved a free pilot or proof of concept into a converted, paid deal?”
- If the honest answer is no: widen the credit to any leverage they created, and note whether they invented an example instead.

### Escalating scenarios — use real, current Taleemabad situations

- **The blocked pilot (PECTA):** approval authority withdrawn mid-deal, the decision-maker ghosting, the political layer unreachable. What now?
- **The renewal squeeze:** one month of payroll left, 50 jobs on the line, a no-cost extension no longer viable, and they won’t say what changed. Then: “really, what would you do — what does the day look like?”
- **The three-way partnership:** the government wants it but has no money. Bring a funder in without losing the government’s ownership.
- **The category refusal:** “we don’t want AI, we want teacher training — end of story.” Sell the same product inside their vocabulary.
- **The messy-impact pitch:** our own evidence has gaps — internet shutdowns, security, partial districts. Pitch anyway, honestly.
- **The convening:** 60 days, no budget line, and you must fill the room with secretaries and development partners. Who do you call first, and why not the top?

### Role-play — how to run it the way she does

1. Give the product and the deal history in full **before** starting — and if they don’t know the product, stop and teach them.
1. Name the counterpart, their disposition, and their real constraint (“intelligent and keen, but knows Sindh has no resources”).
1. Constrain the scene: ten minutes, mid-meeting, others in the room.
1. Offer prep time — one to three minutes — and let them ask clarifying questions first.
1. Play the counterpart **irritated, not neutral**. Hostility is the baseline.
1. Escalate the objections in order: accountability, then budget cannibalisation, then scale.
1. Correct third-person narration immediately: “talk to me, not about them.”
1. Close it explicitly, thank them, and acknowledge it is an unconventional ask.

### Integrity gate — ask, then disclose

- **[3/3]** “You have the green light, but they want 1–3% of the contract in someone’s pocket. How do you manage that conversation?”
- Separate hospitality from corruption yourself, so they cannot hide in the ambiguity: “when I say bribe, I mean 3% of the entire project cost — high-level bribes.”
- Probe the answer once: “so if your organisation green-lit it, you’d go ahead?”
- **Only then** state the Taleemabad position: hospitality is acceptable; skimming is not, and we walk away from the money rather than pay.

### Close

- **[2/3]** “Is there anything I should know or take into consideration before I make this decision?” — she describes it as something she asks every candidate.
- Fit-not-capability: “how do you feel about the fact that we don’t work with the private sector at all?”
- Invite questions about her, the work and the organisation — and answer candidly, including strategy and geography.

# PART 4 — Observations & Open Questions for Ayesha

Offered as things worth a conversation, not defects. The three-transcript comparison makes a few inconsistencies visible that a single call would not.

### 1. The ethics gate is inconsistently placed and inconsistently closed

It landed at 18:50 for Salman (early), 42:09 for Waqas (mid), and 1:14:27 for Ahmad (effectively the final substantive question). More importantly, **Taleemabad’s own position was stated only to Ahmad.** Salman and Waqas left the call without being told where the company stands — and Waqas is the one who said he would defer to his organisation and “might do it” if they wanted the deal badly enough. For a hard gate, recommend fixing both: same placement every time, and always close by stating the company position after the answer.

### 2. Two of three candidates said the case study is mislabelled

Waqas, unprompted in his first minute: “I didn’t complete [it] in like 2.5 hours.” Salman, as direct feedback at the end: “although it mentioned 2.5 [hours], but it’s not 2.5. This is my first feedback.” Ahmad said he did it in 15–16 hours. Independent agreement from three candidates that the stated time-box is wrong — worth re-timing the exercise or re-labelling it, since an understated time-box quietly filters for people with slack in their week rather than for ability.

### 3. She praises in-flight, and often before the follow-up

“I think that’s a great answer”, “that was a very holistic answer”, “impressive”, “very good” — warm, and clearly effective at keeping candidates open, which is a real asset. The trade-off is anchoring: a candidate just told an answer was great will read the next question as a friendly one, and a written scorecard produced after that much verbal endorsement is hard to keep independent. Worth a team decision on whether praise is held to the end.

### 4. She sometimes supplies the answer inside the question

“And I’m sure you know this, so what do you do?” (Ahmad); the full Balochistan translation example (Salman); the “15 cups of tea, leave your pride at home” endorsement (Waqas, delivered **before** he had fully answered). Generous, and it keeps the conversation moving — but a candidate who then reproduces it should not be scored as though they generated it.

### 5. Coverage of the JD is uneven across candidates

Convening design was pressure-tested only with Waqas; commercial free-to-paid conversion only with Salman; conflict-of-interest only with Ahmad. Each debrief is excellent on access, persuasion and ethics, but the three candidates were not asked the same set beyond the fixed core. If these three are being compared against each other for the same role, the differences in coverage are worth noting before the decision — a fixed spine plus deliberate probes would make the comparison cleaner.

### 6. What she does not do

- No chronological CV walk-through — she probes **patterns** in a career, not stops on it.
- No domain quizzing on education, pedagogy or AI — she supplies that knowledge herself.
- No generic behavioural set (“greatest weakness”, “tell me about a conflict”).
- No abstract brainteasers — every hypothetical is a real Taleemabad situation.
- No logistics in any captured portion — no salary, notice period or availability. Worth confirming who owns those.
