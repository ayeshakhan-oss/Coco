Dear Arif,

Thank you for taking the time to speak with us about the Senior Product Manager role at Taleemabad. Your willingness to engage thoughtfully with our questions and share stories from your experience was evident throughout our conversation. We wanted to take a moment to reflect on what we heard and share some observations that might be helpful as you continue your journey.

What We Saw in You

When you talked about the RedConnect integration migration, we felt your genuine commitment to tackling difficult work that others had stepped away from. Every other product manager on your team had declined the project, citing it as too complex and too risky. But you volunteered. And more than that, you stayed with the technical debugging and deployment challenges even when the tourism platform's entire booking workflow depended on getting it right. That kind of persistence in high-stakes situations speaks to something real in your character. You don't walk away when things get hard.

We also noticed your intellectual humility. At Microsoft, when you encountered a junior colleague's superior testing methodology, you didn't defend your own approach. Instead, you learned from her. You adopted her process for mutations and automation, and now you recommend it to others. That willingness to learn from anyone on your team, regardless of seniority, is something we appreciated. And it shows up again in how you describe learning from your QA team about deeper testing practices and now exploring cloud AI certification. There's a genuine appetite for growth there.

Finally, we noticed how thoughtfully you approach delegation. You create the strategic roadmaps, but then you hand projects over to junior product managers for execution and development. You let go so they can grow and gain experience. At scale, that ability to develop others rather than become a bottleneck is critical, and you're already practicing it.

Where We'd Love to Understand More

There were moments in our conversation where we found ourselves wanting to go deeper. When you described covering for a senior developer whose untested code crashed production on the NYC launch day, you shared what happened very clearly. But we were hoping to better understand what that moment felt like from inside. When you realized the severity of what had happened, when you made the decision to step in and fix it, how did you experience that? And beyond solving the technical problem, how did you think about the impact on the developer who made the mistake? We were curious whether there was a moment where you invested in rebuilding their confidence after the crisis passed.

We asked about a valued peer, and you shifted the conversation to "the whole team" rather than sharing a specific relationship. We understood what you meant about team credit and one-on-ones being the best way to surface quiet voices. Those are real practices. But for a leadership role where you'll be managing other product managers, we were hoping to hear about specific individuals you've invested in, celebrated with, built trust with. The kind of relationships where you know each other as complete humans, not just colleagues solving problems together.

In our conversation about difficult conversations, you shared examples about layoffs and receiving feedback about timing and process. Those are high-stakes moments, and you navigated them. But we were hoping to hear about interpersonal conversations common at your level. Giving difficult feedback to a peer about a strategic disagreement. Being vulnerable about a mistake you made. Navigating a situation where someone felt unsupported by you and working to repair that relationship. We were curious how you handle the emotional dimension of those conversations, not just the business logistics.

We also found ourselves wondering about joy. You described yourself as a "smiling emoji" because you think you'll be happy whether you meet deadlines or miss them. That's a positive mindset. And we appreciated hearing that you organize team outings and bowling and meals together because "having good time together is important" to the team. That's thoughtful. But we were hoping to understand what personally brings you joy. You mentioned hiking and mountains and cricket and football. Do those things fuel you? Do they connect to how you show up for your team? Or are they separate from work? For a leader, personal joy sources matter because they determine whether you're drawing from genuine energy or running on discipline and optimism alone.

What We Hope for You

As you move forward, we'd encourage you to notice the moments where you invest deeply in specific individuals. Not just as team members, but as complete people. Who has your trust? Who have you celebrated genuinely? Those relationships are where real team strength lives. And we'd encourage you to reflect on the interpersonal conversations that matter at your level. Not just business decisions, but moments of real human connection where you're honest about challenges and genuinely curious about how the people around you are experiencing things.

We'd also encourage you to connect your personal interests more explicitly to your leadership. What actually energizes you? If hiking and mountains matter to you, how does that shape how you think about teams and what they need? Does that translate into anything about how you build culture or sustain people through hard work?

P.S. We want you to know that what we saw in you was real. Your willingness to tackle hard problems, your openness to learning, your investment in developing others. Those are genuine strengths, and they matter. We hope you'll carry these observations forward and notice where there's room to deepen your investment in the human side of leadership.
